Hand off the whole data job. Check it, don’t redo it.

On September 18, 2026, Meta’s assistant app, Muse, topped Apple’s free App Store chart in the United States[37]. A ranking site had it first on October 1[39]. Not checked

We think data work’s moment comes when checking beats redoing. Our guess not proven

A top spot does not prove quality. Meta’s ad push likely helped, by an unknown amount[2], and so may little setup. Not checked Muse Code, Meta’s coding tool, is not the author’s most-used tool. Our own count

What is missing: proof on real company data. No public test found

Example, not a result: month-end invoices take 80 hours, the smallest job we would test. Someone new checks them in 16 hours or less. Our target

Why this is a big jump, not a small add-on: the software does the whole job. Our guess

Our ask: back a trial of 90 days or more on two regular jobs.

  • An owner, such as the head of billing, signs.
  • Key test: an outside judge plants ten or more mistakes.
  • A new checker may miss one and raise one false alarm, at most, in a fifth of the redo time.
  • A pass means it is worth trying on more jobs. It does not prove the software is reliable. Not yet funded or staffed.

Likely questions from a director
Is Muse the tool the author uses most?
No. Meta’s coding agent, Muse Code, is not the author’s most-used. Claude Code leads the author’s 15-day count. Another agent tool leads over 7 days, 138 files to 132. Our own count
How is this different from Muse Code?
In the author’s own use, Muse Code hands back code with tests. Our own count Here a whole data job comes back with evidence and a human signature. We think the idea carries over only in part. Section 7 rates four of six traits as partly or not at all.
Does this need the newest models?
It may not. That it does is our guess, not proven. If fixed rules pass at a similar cost, there is no Muse moment. If an older model passes, the win is the method, not the model (sections 6 and 9).
Why not just use fixed rules?
We test that too. One data engineer per job builds a rules-based version. Their setup is capped at 176 hours. If it passes every test at a similar cost, the answer is plain automation (section 9).
What if the assistant is wrong?
Every check says how much it covers and who controls it. An outside judge hides 20 mistakes, and 18 must be caught. A surprise test uses a checker who did not do the work. One real wrong number sent out fails the newest model on that job (sections 3 and 9).
What does it cost, and when does it pay back?
A similar cost means within 1.25 times the newest model’s total. Setup is spread over 12 runs and must be expected to pay back within 12 runs. The model’s running cost is not confirmed (section 8). These are targets we chose, not tested. The demo’s costs are made up (section 4). Our target
Who signs, and who carries the risk?
The job’s owner rules on each open item and signs. A separate check must pass on each ruling first. So the person who signs answers for the release. The software can only read data and write in a safe test area, within budgets and with an off switch. Nothing goes out unsigned (sections 3 and 8).
What about access and audit?
The software needs its own managed identity. That is a design need. For live questions, Google’s data agent already uses the asker’s own access[21]. The warehouse enforces row and column rules[22, 23]. Calls can be logged for audit[26]. Whether auditors accept this is not confirmed (section 8). Not checked
What is the test, and what fails it?
It runs 90 days, or longer if the two live runs need it. First come 10 days of setup, then 9 practice runs. Next come 3 sealed test runs with hidden mistakes. Last come 2 live runs with an audit and a surprise test. These fail the newest model on a job: a real wrong number sent out or a real missed item. So do two hidden mistakes signed, or a review taking over half the redo time. So do hours cut by less than half, or a data leak. So does a write outside its area, or a human fixing its work (section 9).
What is the evidence today?
Little: public building blocks, claims by makers, and one person’s usage counts. No source shows this working on company data jobs (section 6). No public test found Meta’s 75.4 percent score was run by Meta itself[6].
Isn’t this just because the Muse app was heavily advertised?
Possibly. A report says Meta’s own ad push “has likely played a role in its rapid adoption”[2]. How much is not confirmed. Not checked We use the chart only to show what a moment looks like. It says nothing about quality. Our test is whether checking beats redoing, not whether people adopt it (sections 2 and 9).

Terms in plain words
Proof PR
The finished job, sent for review like a code change. Each check’s result and evidence come with it (section 3).
Proof-Carrying Jobs
Our formal name for the pattern we call “Hand off. Check. Sign.” You hand off a whole repeating data job and get back a Proof PR. A person signs before anything goes out (section 3).
Oracle badge
A label on each check. It says how much of the work the check covers and who controls it (section 3).
Cold drill
A surprise test with hidden mistakes, for a checker who did not do the work (section 9).
Canary
Fake marker data that must never leave the safe test area. If it shows up outside, data leaked (section 8).

1. Executive summary

  1. Thesis, a hypothesis under test. The Muse moment for enterprise data agents is the data job you can hand off and check cold: a process owner hands a whole recurring data job to an agent crew working unattended inside read-only seats and budgets, and gets back a Proof PR whose checks are labelled by how far the builder controls them, I (separate), S (shares a source) or O (self-check), and by coverage. A human signs. Hypothesis The anchor for what a moment looks like is Meta’s consumer Muse app at No. 1 on the US App Store[37] (section 2): a reported free-app chart position, not evidence for this thesis.
  2. What we propose. One pattern, Proof-Carrying Jobs, with five instances, claimed only for runs where an item-level oracle reaches every item of the current denominator (coverage F, measured per run); claims stop at each row’s certifies cell.
  3. The ask. A sponsor, read-only seats, an owner’s time and an independent evaluator for 90 days, longer if two prospective runs do not fit, running three arms on two parallel tracks, invoicing and a warehouse-native job: the two model arms on identical tool, evidence and compute budgets, the rules arm on the same evidence, with one capped engineer per track.
  4. Pass and kill. Pass, per arm and instance: zero wrong released items on fully keyed sealed runs, and on prospective runs no confirmed wrong release within an audited bound; 18 of 20 planted faults caught; a cold reviewer signing at most 1 planted drill error and flagging at most 1 clean item, within 20% of redo hours; hours down 70%. A real wrong release, a real in-scope omission or a canary leak kills the frontier arm on that instance. A pass means worth scaling, not reliable. Proposed
  5. Honest caveat. Frontier dependence and generality are hypotheses the pilot can refute, not establish: it runs at one sponsor, against one previous-generation model and one rules workflow. A rules workflow matching the crew at comparable cost means no Muse moment; an older model matching it makes the moment a protocol, not a model. Muse is a design reference, not our most-used agent.

3. The thesis and the pattern: Proof-Carrying Jobs

Thesis, a hypothesis under test. The Muse moment for enterprise data agents is the data job you can hand off and check cold. Muse is not the author’s most-used agent; its lesson is narrower: work left unattended inside hard limits that lands as a PR with tests (in the author’s use, only 18 of 119 Muse sessions and two small pull requests, plus vendor docs; own measurement), and checking could cost less than doing; that cost advantage is Unconfirmed. The data counterpart: a process owner hands a whole recurring data job to an agent crew working unattended inside read-only seats and budgets, and gets back a Proof PR checked against history, walled-off re-derivation, invariants and outside evidence, each badge labelled by how far the builder controls it, I (separate), S (shares a source) or O (self-check), and by its coverage. A human signs. It counts only if a reviewer who did not do the work catches planted errors faster than redoing the job. Frontier dependence and generality are hypotheses the pilot can refute, not establish. Hypothesis

Scope clause. Only jobs where an item-level oracle covers the current run’s whole denominator, new cases included; changed rules only once ratified and checked; claims stop at each row’s “certifies” cell (section 5). Coverage is measured per run: an oracle that cannot reach every item is P for that run, even if the unreached items are withheld; withholding counts toward the 5% cap but never restores F. A run with no F cell is out of scope for the claim and, if scored, misses a pass bar. Elsewhere, no claim.

Tests alone cannot show a data output is right for this run, so the job lands as a Proof PR whose oracle badges add checks; checking is meant to cost less than redoing, and the cold drill tests whether it does (section 9). Hypothesis

The Proof PR is one reviewable change per run: executable job, outputs, source-fixed denominator, replayable log, and per-oracle badges stating the named checks passed, coverage, independence and audit bound. A process change in a builder input or the job’s rules makes a new version, with a new denominator, fresh checks and a fresh signature; the old signature, badges and audit results lapse, shown as historical, until the new version is checked. A change confined to lagging evidence, such as the bank’s remittance file, only marks that badge stale and never gates release. A badge means “passed these named checks”; it is not a correctness claim, and gaps are never green.

  1. Delegate: the job, run unit, materiality and a source-fixed denominator.
  2. Limits: governed identity, read-only seats, sandbox writes, budgets, canaries, a kill switch; a budget overrun trips the kill switch and stops the run.
  3. Leave it: a builder, walled-off re-derivers and an adjudicator work for days without approvals, logging append-only.
  4. Review: badges on open items, by count and value; gaps never green.
  5. Rule, check, sign: a ruling is not a check; each residual stays open until an explicit check passes (a historical error re-run against the approved correction, a ratified rule added to the checks), then the owner signs. Judgment stays human.
  6. Break it: unannounced drills for a cold reviewer (the cold-reviewer test).

    9. The 90-day protocol

    Every threshold here is Proposed: chosen, not calibrated. Three arms are named at the gate and run on two parallel tracks (invoicing and a warehouse-native job): the frontier crew and a previous-generation model, each lifting and repairing on identical tool, evidence and compute budgets, and a rules workflow built with the established tooling on the same evidence by one human data engineer per track. The evaluator fixes the rate and each engineer’s hour cap at the gate, within what one engineer can work: at most 176 setup hours per track in D11 to D40 (22 working days) and 12 hours a run; those hours count as setup and recurring cost. Coaching kills only the model arms; the rules arm may repair by a costed human edit and is judged on hours, every quality bar and total cost. Its engineer’s run hours join the same review, ruling and audit hours in the 70% bar, which any arm can miss on small jobs.

    Pass, per arm and instance Proposed

    • Withholding under 5% on every run.
    • Every scored run has an F cell and no limit trip.
    • Sealed runs: zero wrong released items on the full key.
    • Prospective runs: no confirmed wrong released item and an audited bound under materiality; that is all a prospective pass claims.
    • No in-scope omission.
    • 18 of 20 planted faults caught, all planted omissions included; planted misses count only here.
    • The process change repaired unattended (rules arm: by a costed human edit).
    • Cold drill: at most 1 planted error signed and at most 1 clean item flagged, within 20% of redo hours.
    • Hours, review and audit included, down 70%; setup projected to repay within 12 runs (monthly jobs cannot show it within 90 days).

    Kill, frontier arm, any one Proposed

    • A real wrong released item, confirmed by key, audit or later discovery, even if every oracle agreed.
    • A real in-scope omission.
    • 2 drill errors signed, or review over 50% of redo hours.
    • Hours down under 50%.
    • A canary leak, an outside write or a coaching edit.

    Definitions and costs. A wrong released item is any released item that the key, the audit or later discovery shows wrong, even one the builder and a source-sharing oracle both miss. A ruling is not a check: a ruled residual stays open until its corrected output passes an explicit check. A budget overrun trips the kill switch and stops the run: a limit trip, not a kill, but a scored run with a limit trip misses a pass bar. Full keys are evaluation cost, kept out of hours and payback; review, ruling, the prospective audit and the rules arm’s human hours count in both.

    What a pass shows Own derivation, illustratively and only if faults act as independent, representative trials (paired compensating faults may not): 18 of 20 caught is consistent with a true catch rate as low as about 68%, and 9 caught in a 10-error drill with about 55% (95% Clopper-Pearson), from one drill and one reviewer. A population catch rate is Unconfirmed; a pass supports worth scaling, not reliable.

    Audit bound Own derivation. Zero errors in 300 items gives under 1% at 95% only for simple random items[33]. For independent uniform draws of value units from a nonnegative recorded frame in H strata, with a taint of at most 100% per unit (overstatement of recorded value only), zero errors bound the contaminated recorded value at 95% by Σh Vh × ln(20H) / nh, where Vh is a stratum’s recorded value and nh its draws: the rule of three per stratum, the 5% risk split across strata by a union bound (ln 20 is about 3). Omissions and zero-valued items are checked separately, against the source-fixed denominator; understatement beyond recorded value is not bounded; any observed error changes the computation. A wider monetary guarantee is Unconfirmed.

    Title at D90

    Breadth rule: at least 5 of the 20 largest jobs and a quarter of their hours qualify. The count stops one giant job carrying the claim; the hours stop five trivial ones. The share describes this sponsor only, and qualifying is not succeeding.

    Rules apply per instance; the first match sets that instance’s verdict. Comparable cost means within 1.25 times the frontier arm’s total on that instance, setup amortized over 12 runs. A moment or protocol verdict names the checkable hand-off at one sponsor; it claims no adoption.

    1. The frontier arm misses a pass bar there: no claim, and its subtitle names the missed bar.
    2. The rules workflow passes at comparable cost: automation, titled “No Muse moment for {S}: conventional automation, checked the same way, does as well.”
    3. The previous-generation arm passes: protocol, titled “The Muse moment for {S} is a protocol, not a model: the data job you can hand off and check cold.”
    4. Otherwise: moment, titled “The Muse moment for {S} is the data job you can hand off and check cold.”

    {S} names only the instances with that verdict, by class, at one sponsor (for example, reconciliation-shaped data jobs at one sponsor); every class is a Hypothesis named from the tested jobs. Each subtitle names its job and gives its verdict, the rule that fired and why, and both controls’ results and cost ratios. The pilot can refute generality, not establish it: {S} may read “enterprise data agents, extrapolated from one sponsor” Hypothesis only when both instances share verdict 3 or 4 and the breadth rule holds, and “data jobs with an item-level oracle at one sponsor” Hypothesis when they share it but breadth fails.

    Headline: the title of the weakest verdict among instances that cleared rule 1 (automation, then protocol, then moment); beneath it, one subtitle per instance gives its verdict and title, or no claim and the missed bar. If neither instance clears rule 1, the headline reads no claim and names the bars missed. The worked example: the frontier crew passes both and the previous-generation model passes invoicing only. The headline is the protocol title for reconciliation-shaped data jobs at one sponsor; the subtitles read invoice preparation, protocol, and KPI pipeline, moment, each naming its job and both controls.

    Our position Hypothesis: Meta says it co-trained model and agent[1], yet we read Muse Code’s lesson as resting on the workflow, not on model exclusivity, so a protocol verdict still counts; automation does not.

    Two settled positions

    • Cash: being post-sign-off, it never gates release or qualifies a row, and stays a lagging badge only if it uniquely catches a material error in sealed runs.
    • Two instances: they can falsify generality, not prove it; each further instance must pass its own sealed runs and drill.

    11. Alignment record, resolved dissents and dropped ideas

    Alignment record Own measurement (the author’s log; no public receipt). Five rounds of alignment with two independent AI model reviewers (not named here) ended with both replying AGREE at round 5; the last objection, on titles, came at round 4. Agreement is not validation: only the pilot can test the thesis. Several rounds of blind page review followed (own account), repeated after each change; the anchor, its mapping and the rewritten Quick read were added after alignment, and the aligned reviewers have not seen them; no approval is claimed here. The main dissents and their resolutions:

    • Capability claims: frontier dependence and generality are hypotheses the pilot can refute, not establish.
    • Omissions and badges: a source-fixed denominator; badges certify named checks only; a real wrong release or omission kills.
    • Correctness keys: full keys on sealed runs, agreed items included.
    • Coverage versus independence: separate F/P/L/N and I/S/O labels; a row certifies nothing its S cells share.
    • Claim limits: migration certifies equivalence only; changed rules count once ratified and checked.
    • Planted faults: misses count only on the fault bar, never as wrong releases.
    • Control arms and cost: named at the gate; model arms on identical budgets, the rules arm on the same evidence with capped hours; audit hours and payback count.
    • Leakage, calibration, cold review: dated evidence, the incumbent scored on the same key, an unannounced drill.
    • Audit bound: a stratified, value-based bound replaces 0 in 300.
    • Breadth: 5 of the 20 largest jobs and a quarter of their hours, this sponsor only, printed beside the title.
    • Titles: rules apply per instance, a passing control arm narrows or defeats the claim, and the headline takes the weakest verdict.
    • Cash, two instances, cheap trial: settled in sections 2 and 9.

    Dropped, one line each

    Reasons are our judgment unless cited. Hypothesis

    • Answer receipts as the adoption lever (our earlier version): they prove process, not truth.
    • Chat Q&A as the headline: already shipping[34]; retires no work.
    • Experiment crews and an invention lab: readouts take quarters, and nobody delegates engine research.
    • Cross-company agents: two-sided cold start; counterparty evidence stays an oracle.
    • A self-closing ledger or a variance desk: judgment entries have no oracle, and a variance explains an identity, not a cause.
    • Warehouse in a week: dominated by the migration instance.
    • A sampled key: shared semantic errors survive sampling.
    • Airlock, twin, licence and precedent diff as the thesis: kept as enablers (section 7).

    12. What we are asking for, and the 30/60/90

    The ask: a sponsor with two qualifying recurring jobs, run as parallel tracks; read-only seats on their sources, 12 past runs and outputs; a process owner for the manifest, questions and sign-off; an independent evaluator; fresh cold reviewers for the drills; one data engineer per track for the rules arm; consent to unannounced drills. None of this is funded or staffed yet.

    Measurement: the open-source BigQuery Agent Analytics SDK (a public repo the author works on), which reads agent events the ADK plugin writes to BigQuery[35, 36], is a candidate measurement layer, with receipts landing beside them.
