1. ReplayRCA: Measuring Post-hoc Diagnosis and Live Oversight of Agent Failures by Counterfactual Replay — https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.41025

Full PDF read (pdftotext of the complete paper, all parts read including §1–§8, references, and Appendices A–G; key numbers below cite tables/sections as printed).

What it is. A benchmark protocol, not a detector: given a recorded failed run, a diagnoser proposes one (step, replacement action); the environment is restored to the exact prior state (every replayed observation verified byte-for-byte, §3.1), the replacement executes, and the same worker continues k = 5 times. The score, causal repair (Eq. 1, §3.2), subtracts a paired placebo that replays the worker's own action through the identical mechanism, removing spontaneous recovery and restart effects. Two tracks share runs and metric (§3.3): post-hoc autopsy (diagnoser sees the whole run, told it failed) and live oversight (supervisor sees each irreversible action before it executes, may intervene once, never told the outcome; succeeding runs mixed in; each intervention charged λ = 5 points). Tasks come from OpsWorld, a deterministic procedural back-office world (15 tools, 3 families, 6 levels, level 6 multimodal; §4, Appendix A), with an executable checker and no model judge anywhere in scoring. Items are harvested systematic failures calibrated so retained failures spontaneously succeed only 3.5% on average (§4.1: 62 failures + 41 successes survive of 143 candidates; oversight uses 62 + 25 = 87 runs).

Results that matter (with receipts). Autopsy (Table 1, §5.1): best causal repair +51.3 points [+40, +63]; 0 of 78 pairwise differences survive Holm correction, so the top is a cluster, not a hierarchy; the random-step placebo sits at −0.6 despite 27.7% raw recovery — the placebo is doing real work. Label agreement ranks differently: scoring by consensus step instead would put a different model first (87% agreement) with rank correlation only 0.43 to causal repair (Table 12, Appendix E). The last-step shortcut that dominates label-based suites fails under replay: forced-last-step scores +28.1 vs +45.2 free choice, −17.1 points, p = 0.004 (§5.6, Table 6). Oversight (Table 3, §5.3): the two leading supervisors (+29.1 and +27.5 net) are not separable, and one is a small open-weight model; false alarms are rare except for one supervisor (8/25). Explaining ≠ preventing (Table 5, §5.4): of four models run in both roles, three show no detectable gap, but the model among the best post-hoc (+45.2) is the weakest live (+30.6), paired Δ −14.5 [−23, −5], p = 0.002 — it intervenes early with "read something" actions (81% vs 41–48% for peers) instead of redoing the faulty step. Multimodal level 6 is only modestly harder (+39.3 vs +47.0 pooled, §5.5) because the fix is often just "open the attachment." Honest limits (§7): 62 items give ±10-point intervals (84 items needed to separate models 10 points apart, Appendix E); one simulated environment; 41/62 items from a single worker; one vendor family ran below top reasoning effort, so its ranks are lower bounds (the effort ablation is worth +15.2 points to its smallest model, §5.6); self-renewal from stronger workers is designed but untested — the pilot harvested zero calibrated failures from a 92%-success worker.

Why BQAA cares. BQAA stores exactly the artifact this protocol consumes: ordered tool-call rows per run. The transferable claim is methodological — any "root cause" column, failure cluster, or auto-diagnosis feature should be validated by counterfactual continuation net of a placebo replay, not by agreement with annotators or other models (ρ = 0.43). It also warns against picking a live guardrail/supervisor model by post-hoc explanation quality: on identical runs those rankings inverted for one model of four. Caveat for the card: causal repair credits effective intervention, not earliest cause (§6) — a repair at the refund step and a repair at the skipped-policy step both count. That is the right standard for operators, but a warehouse that also wants etiology needs attribution labels in addition, not instead.

2. What Standard Evaluations Hide About Detectors of Hijacked LLM Agents: Rare Attacks, Unseen Tools and Benchmark Transfer — https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.41803

Full PDF read (complete paper read including Sections I–IX, Tables I–VI, Figures 1–4 as extracted, and Appendices A–D).

What it is. An evaluation audit plus controlled measurements, explicitly not a new detector. The audit (Table I) covers 11 trace-detector studies: mixed-class test sets run 16.5–86.3% attacks/anomalies, only four report a threshold-free measure, none varies the attack share, six report no cross-benchmark or within-target test, and only one evaluates adaptive attackers. The authors then train simple detectors (behavioral logistic regression, combined tool-count + behavior, a GRU over tool sequences, a step-embedding reimplementation, a TF-IDF content reference) plus a released few-shot LLM action monitor run with two backbones, on a synthetic step-labeled corpus (12,536 trajectories, 103 tools) and recorded runs from a prompt-injection benchmark (2,294 compromised / 392 benign traces after filtering; §III, Appendix B). Thresholds are always fixed on training data (θr80, Eq. 6); thresholds are never re-tuned on test data (Fig. 1).

Results that matter (with receipts). Rare attacks (§IV, Table III): the combined detector's AUROC 0.879 becomes precision 0.076 at 2% prevalence (12.2 false alerts per true alert, 203 alerts per 1,000 sessions); the sequence model does better (AUROC 0.936, precision 0.238) but its test recall at the training threshold is 0.682, short of the 0.80 the threshold targets. Precision depends on the negative mix as much as on prevalence: 0.117 vs benign only, 0.076 with 27% hard negatives, 0.039 vs hard negatives only. Only the content reference keeps precision above 0.5 at 2% — and §VI-B shows that is a generator-template artifact. Unseen tools (§V, Fig. 3, Table IV): holding out worlds or tasks costs nothing distinguishable, but masking tool names at test time drops the combined detector 0.879→0.787 and the GRU 0.936→0.674; both pairwise advantages cross zero between coverage 0.775 and 0.483, i.e. rankings reverse when roughly half the tools are new. The mechanism is untrained inputs (zero tool block mapped to a constant offset of +1.65 to +1.90 logits; untrained unknown-token embedding), and tool-input dropout largely removes the reversal at no in-distribution cost (GRU-drop 0.943 vs 0.936). Thresholds fail more broadly: even the step-embedding detector, which has no untrained input, sees held-out negatives shifted +5.2 to +7.4 logits and FPR rising from 0.001 to 0.046–0.106 (§V-E). Transfer (§VI, Table V): every detector trained on the synthetic corpus ranks the recorded benchmark at chance (0.473–0.520), including representations reaching 0.956–0.996 when trained within the target; both benchmarks are near-separable by surface content (generator templates; five fixed attack strings alone give AUROC 0.998 within the target). The untrained monitor does transfer (0.930 [0.909, 0.951]) but its native decision with the open backbone still implies precision 0.096 at 2% (§VI-C); with the commercial backbone the interval reaches above 0.5 on one benchmark and 0.054 on the other — usability depends on backbone and benchmark, and the authors' own registered prediction M3 failed for that backbone (Table VI, Appendix D). A length cue the attacker writes (mean observation length) carries much of the surviving behavioral signal; padding the injected observation by 1,200 characters collapses recall (0.771→0.033 combined; 0.682→0.000 GRU; §V-F). Limits (§VIII): one benchmark pair in one direction, small benign pool (392), no adaptive attacker, most analysis plans not prospective (only the monitor's was hash-registered), and the reimplementation's numbers are not the published detector's.

Why BQAA cares. This is a reporting-standards paper for any score computed over stored traces. The seven recommendations (§VII-B) translate directly into warehouse-view requirements: report AUROC with prevalence-weighted precision at stated attack rates (1–10%); state the negative mix behind every precision figure and FPR by negative class; report tool coverage κ per split and include a mostly-new-tools split; fix thresholds on training data and show their behavior per test set (train tool-indexed inputs with dropout); include a dumb content baseline to expose template separability and perturbation-test attacker-controlled cues like observation length; pair cross-benchmark claims with a within-target control; and judge untrained monitors by their native decision at stated prevalence, not by ranking alone. A BQAA anomaly view that shows AUROC without prevalence, κ, and threshold provenance is, by this paper's measurements, hiding the three ways the score will fail in production.

3. How Much Can Tool Calls Reveal About You? Privacy Leakage from Tool-Call Traces — https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.41885

Full PDF read (complete paper read including §1–§7, Tables 1–8, Figures 1–12 as extracted, and Appendices A–D with scoring rules, calibration sources, and prompt skeletons).

What it is. PrivacyTrace, a benchmark for user-profile inference from MCP tool-call traces by semi-honest observers who never see the conversation (§3: local observer = one tool server's calls; global observer = full trace, linkable across sessions by persistent identity, Eq. 2). Construction (§4): 1,000 synthetic profiles sampled to fit 25 population margins from official aggregates via iterative proportional fitting (Appendix B, Tables 4–5; final max marginal L1 discrepancy 0.0156), each with 17 ground-truth attributes (Appendix A.3, Table 3, including four synthetic exact identifiers); profile-conditioned tasks in four domains (travel, health, shopping, career learning) executed through real MCP tools from 25 servers, yielding 4,000 trajectories and 67,950 tool calls. Attackers are forced-prediction LLM profilers scored against a schema-only prior baseline, in one-shot and two-stage (select trace-supported attributes, then complete) variants (§3.3, §4.2); failed/unparseable generations stay in the denominator (§5.1).

Results that matter (with receipts). Trace access lifts average attack success from 17.97% (prior) to 26.25% on single-domain views (+8.28 points; §5.2, Fig. 4a). Observer scope compounds: pooled ASR 24.24% local-server → 26.25% single-domain → 32.77% linked four-domain (Fig. 4b), with a properly paired +3.09-point local→domain gain on a common slot population (Appendix C.3) — clues are spread across servers, so whoever can join them profiles better. Leakage is consistent across four attacker models (25.29–28.32%, Table 1), so it is a property of the traces, not of one profiler. Attribute recovery is broad but not identifying: citizenship 57.73%, religious belief 50.03%, ethnicity 49.85%, current location 38.40% (Appendix C.5), while exact identifiers land only 35 correct of 16,000 slots (34 names, 1 phone, 0 emails, 0 government ids; §5.2). By domain, shopping leaks most (31.86%), then travel (28.33%), health (23.25%), career learning (21.57%). Channel ablation (§5.3, Fig. 6) is the operational core: metadata + call sequence alone scores 14.53% — below prior guessing — while adding arguments reaches 27.71% (+13.18) and adding returned results 24.54% (+10.01); the full trace (26.25%) sits between, so results add nothing net once arguments are visible. Higher-leakage servers are the semantic ones (geocoding, flight search, web search, weather, time; Fig. 12, Appendix C.3). Two-stage inference nearly matches one-shot (25.83% vs 26.25%; Stage 1 selects 17.06% of slots at 45.90% precision; conditioning on those selections lifts completion of the rest by +3.30 points over matched prior guessing; §5.4, Fig. 7, Table 8). Visible-call volume correlates with ASR (20.87% at 1–5 calls → 29.26% at 21–40; Fig. 4c, Table 6) but the authors correctly label this association, not causation. Limits (§6): synthetic profiles/tasks (calibration matches margins, not behavioral realism); no consented real-user validation; mitigations (query obfuscation, content minimization, session isolation) proposed but untested.

Why BQAA cares. A trace warehouse is the global observer in this threat model: it holds the joined, retained, cross-domain view that profiles best. Three schema-level consequences follow. First, treat tool-argument and tool-result payload columns as personal-data-bearing by default — they carry essentially all of the measured leakage, while tool names, order, and counts alone profile worse than priors; redaction/tokenization effort spent on metadata instead of payloads is misallocated. Second, the join key is a privacy control: linking sessions/domains under one persistent user id is what moves ASR from ~24% toward ~33%, so retention windows and cross-domain linkage belong in the same policy conversation as payload access. Third, "no exact identifiers recovered" is not a safety claim: citizenship, religion, ethnicity, and location are recoverable at 38–58% from legitimate traces alone, which is the attribute set privacy reviews actually care about. Synthetic-data caveat applies to magnitudes, not to the channel ordering, which is the part BQAA should build on.

Six-week EV

  • Adopt placebo-relative scoring for any diagnosis feature (from Rank 1). Cheapest first step: for stored failed runs, log (a) the proposed decisive step, (b) continuation success after the proposed fix where replay/simulation is feasible, and (c) the paired no-fix continuation rate. Report the difference, never the raw fix rate — in ReplayRCA's pool raw recovery (27.7%) would have flattered every diagnoser, and label agreement (ρ = 0.43 to repair) would have crowned the wrong model. Where true replay is infeasible in production, the same discipline applies offline on sampled failures before any "root cause" column ships.
  • Make detector scorecards prevalence- and coverage-aware (from Rank 2). Any hijack/anomaly view over BQAA traces should carry, per score: assumed attack prevalence with precision at 1%/2%/10%, FPR split by negative class (benign vs hard-negative vs failed-attack), tool coverage κ of the evaluated population vs training, threshold provenance (fixed on training data, date, target recall), and a content-baseline comparison. Add tool-input dropout (or an explicit unknown-tool path) to any tool-indexed model before trusting it on tenants with new tool catalogs — rankings inverted at ~50% new tools, and thresholds drifted even without untrained inputs, so schedule threshold re-validation on local traffic as an operational task, not a launch task.
  • Govern payloads and linkage, not metadata (from Rank 3). Prioritize argument/result payload minimization, field-level redaction, and shorter raw-payload retention over any metadata scrubbing; put cross-session/cross-domain user linkage behind the same approval as payload access, since linkage is what compounds inference (24.24% → 32.77%). For privacy reviews, quote attribute-level recoverability (citizenship/religion/ethnicity/location at 38–58% in synthetic traces), not identifier recovery (35/16,000), as the risk measure — and re-measure on consented internal traces before treating the synthetic magnitudes as production numbers.
