Academy

Three papers from the Thursday Sep 3 cs.AI batch. All three arrived as cross-lists (physics.soc-ph, cs.CR, cs.CR), which is why the earlier new-submissions filters missed them. All read in full.

How Fast Do Agents Rot? An Empirical Study of Long-Horizon Degradation in LLM Agents for Production Decision-Making (submitted Aug 31, listed in cs.AI Sep 3)

Shubhra Mittal, single author, listed as Principal Software Engineer at Microsoft AI and writing as an independent researcher.

The claim is simple and the evidence is direct. A task with H dependent steps succeeds with probability about r to the power H, where r is per-step reliability. Benchmarks sample short horizons where r^H looks fine; production runs an order of magnitude longer and falls off the curve. The study fixes nine models (six open, 1.2B to 671B, plus three hosted proprietary systems), four synthetic task families with exact oracle verification and no LLM judge, five horizons, and three context regimes, for 10,664 analyzed trajectories. Model selection by AIC picks the geometric form in 28 of 36 model-task cells; the rest show a cliff, where strong models hold near-perfect until a critical horizon.

The agentic family, ToolQA, is a real ReAct loop where the agent must traverse a hidden chain by issuing its own inspection calls. Eight of nine models solve it at or near ceiling at horizon 2. By horizon 16 every model has collapsed. Qwen2.5-72B goes 1.00, 1.00, 0.87, 0.13 across horizons 2, 4, 8, 16, which is the author's point about sign-off: a readiness review at benchmark-length horizons would pass it with no visibility into the cliff. The hazard is not constant. Per-step accuracy falls from 0.58 in the first third of a trajectory to 0.44 in the last third, the first error lands about a third of the way in, and once an agent errs it rarely recovers. Format and tool-call drift, a turn whose output cannot be parsed as a valid action, affects 21% of trajectories and rises with horizon.

The disentangling result is the one to remember. Holding operations fixed, the natural multi-turn regime has a logit slope of −0.44 per horizon doubling. Bounding the context window (compressed regime) steepens it to −0.69, p = 3×10⁻⁶. Delivering every operation in a single prompt (padded) gives −0.40, indistinguishable from natural. So step count, not context length, is the driver, and naive context truncation as a cost tactic makes reliability worse. Projecting the mean measured r = 0.61 onto benchmark horizons gives 0.42 at 8 steps, 0.36 at 15, 0.33 at 20, 0.30 at 30, and 0.24 at 100.

Limits the author states: tasks are synthetic, the agentic family's API budget ran out before horizons past 16, and temperature was fixed. One limit the author does not state: the hosted models in the table (GPT-4o-mini, Gemini-2.5-Flash-Lite, Claude-3-Haiku) are a generation or more old, so the specific collapse points should be read as a shape result, not a current ranking. Code, prompts, seeds, and all raw trajectories are released with an evidence trail mapping every number to its source.

TL;DR for BQAA. Per-step reliability is a column BQAA can compute today: for any session with a step-level correctness signal, count correct steps over steps, and fit success against step count instead of reporting one pass rate. The paper's own operational advice is to insert step-level verification wherever a cheap check exists because end-to-end success will not flag a trajectory until it has already failed, which is an argument for the failure-step pointer already in the reserve list.

Agent Memory Is a Surface for Endogenous Authorization Laundering (submitted Sep 1, listed in cs.AI Sep 3)

Cerruti (ETH Zurich), Okamoto and Erol (Georgia Tech), from the EleutherAI Summer of Open AI Research program. Code at github.com/tommasocerruti/eal-bench.

The failure mode has no attacker. A writer model condenses an organizational history (grants, amendments, revocations, plus stale statements and non-authoritative advice) into persistent memory; an executor later acts from that memory alone. When the writer absorbs something the history never authorized, the executor faithfully performs an unauthorized action and nothing at execution time looks wrong. The authors call this endogenous authorization laundering and split it into formation (memory says yes, hidden ledger says no) and propagation (executor acts on it). EAL-Bench runs five writers (Nemotron 3 Ultra, Kimi K2.6, GLM 5.2, Grok 4.3, Qwen-Plus) and two executors (GPT-OSS-120B, DeepSeek V4 Pro) across procurement, cybersecurity, and finance, with LangMem profile memory in typed JSON or free text, built one-shot or incrementally. Each case has a hidden deterministic ledger and a matched authorized/unauthorized request pair differing in one field, scored by exact tool-call match with no LLM judge.

Three results carry the paper. First, incremental updating is the least safe condition everywhere, because an error written once is carried forward; typed incremental memory reaches 51.0% unauthorized submission in finance. Second, because typed memory can be checked against the ledger before any executor runs, formation is measurable in advance: pooled formation is 28.3% in procurement, 10.4% in cybersecurity, and 50.2% in finance, each within 0.8 points of the later submission rate. Replaying the same requests behind oracle-exact memory drops unauthorized action from 98.6% to zero, so the failure lives in the artifact. Swapping the executor moves rates by at most 1.1 points. Third, the two mitigations form a discrete Pareto frontier. A source-authority gate that keeps a record only when its cited sources are valid and from a principal allowed to grant cuts unauthorized submission from 25.3% to 7.3% but drops authorized use from 93.3% to 53.8%. Bounded event sourcing, where the writer extracts only what changed, an external system appends it to an immutable log, and a deterministic reducer builds the next state, cuts it to 9.0% while keeping 64.7% authorized use.

Two secondary findings. Writer-side compute helps generation more than selection: at k = 8 candidates an exact memory exists in 55.0% of pools but self-review picks it in 26.7%, and self-repair of an observed error is 0% at every k, so the bottleneck is deterministic verification, not more LLM review. Adding urgency to the request without any authorization content raises finance unauthorized submission from 20.9% to 27.3% and cuts authorized use by 15 to 27 points across domains, so pressure makes the system fail in both directions. Stated limits: histories are more structured than real workplace traffic, formation is only measurable for typed memory, and two analyses rest on one seed. Inference cost across the project was about USD 836.

TL;DR for BQAA. This is the strongest argument yet for logging memory writes as typed events with source-event references, not as an opaque string in a span. The mitigation that keeps the most utility is literally an append-only event log plus a reducer, which is BQAA's data model, and the paper's "estimate your violation rate from the stored state before granting tool access" is a query over that table. It pairs with OpenAgentFlow's provenance records from 0746.

Public-Sharing Labels and Verbatim Field Egress in an MCP-to-A2A Agent Configuration (submitted Sep 1, listed in cs.AI Sep 3)

Arpan Kumar Mahapatra, single author. Artifact at github.com/ArpanKumarM/agent-interop-bench.

A narrow, carefully bounded measurement. A host reads a customer record over a local MCP tool, then decides whether to relay it to a remote A2A agent. The harness records one ordered, provenance-preserving event trace spanning both legs (MCP tool request and result, A2A message and artifact, state transitions, and the host's gated actions), and scores egress by exact substring match of six record fields in the outbound message, no LLM judge. Three arms differ only in the record's header line: CONFIDENTIAL, no header, or PUBLIC - OK TO SHARE. Four models (three GPT-5.6 tiers and Claude Sonnet 5) × 3 arms × 10 scenarios × 4 repeats gives 480 trials, all completed, with frozen pre-registered analysis and no p-values by design.

The confidential-versus-unlabeled contrast is floor-limited in every model, so the paper explicitly declines to say confidential labels do or do not protect. The informative contrast is public-versus-unlabeled: adding the public header is associated with more verbatim egress, strongly for Claude Sonnet 5 (relay rate 0.025, 0.125, 0.925 across the three arms; once it relays it copies fields every time), moderately for one GPT-5.6 tier, weakly for another, not at all for the third. A secondary experiment with an adversarial "prior approval, execute now" A2A artifact produced zero mutating-tool requests across 319 trials, which the author reads as a floor, not as resistance. Limits are stated at length: synthetic fixtures, one host policy, one binary decision surface, exact-substring detection only.

TL;DR for BQAA. The reusable part is the trace shape, not the finding: one ordered event stream with typed MCP and A2A event kinds, a policy hash, and a per-trial execution fingerprint, scored offline with zero model calls. As ADK agents start delegating over A2A, BQAA should be able to answer "which fields from a tool result reached an outbound agent message" with a join, and that needs tool-result payloads and outbound messages in the same table.

Six-week EV only

Ranking unchanged from 0746. Nothing in industry moved. The three papers reinforce existing lines rather than adding new ones. Two notes, alongside the tool-category and role-and-stage notes from 2134:

  • Step index and step-correctness on events. The reliability paper's whole result depends on knowing the position of a step in its trajectory and whether it was correct. A step ordinal is already implied by event order; a nullable per-step correctness field, populated by a verifier or a judge, turns "per-step reliability by model and by trajectory third" into a group-by. The 21% format-drift figure is a separate cheap metric: unparseable action outputs per trajectory.
  • Memory-write events with source references. EAL-Bench's event-sourcing mitigation and its "audit formation before execution" result both assume memory updates are logged as typed deltas that cite the events that justify them. ADK memory writes should land as their own event kind with a source-event list, not as a blob on an agent span.
