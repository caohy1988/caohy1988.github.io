NEW 1 — Claude Enterprise inference hooks graduate to GA; measure the coverage of inline enforcement

Dated catch-up, not a confirmed in-window launch. The Oct 20 release notes explicitly say inference hooks are out of beta. The prior brief held this item because there was no reliable publication time relative to 12:34 PT. That clock remains unresolved. The live catalog has no card for this capability, so this admission uses the permitted never-carded catch-up path; it does not claim the launch occurred after the cutoff.

The product documentation describes an Enterprise organization sending a governed request's transcript to its HTTPS security service before inference. The service returns allow or deny; an optional tool-call event can also gate a response's proposed tool calls before execution. The default verdict timeout is five seconds. A configured failure policy determines whether endpoint errors/timeouts allow or block requests, and sustained endpoint failures trigger a circuit breaker. Shadow mode, rollout sampling and role exclusions make enforcement coverage variable.

This is a material product capability because it creates an inline decision and telemetry boundary across governed Enterprise conversations. Its scope is bounded: Claude Platform API organizations, Amazon Bedrock and Google Cloud are excluded; raw attachments/images, system prompts and internal context are not sent; some internal tool calls and hosted feature model calls are outside coverage. Verdicts allow or deny; they do not rewrite content. BQAA implication: join verdicts to the applicable coverage and failure-policy state. A missing denial is uninterpretable without knowing whether that request was inspected, sampled out, exempt, or allowed during an endpoint failure.

Progress — Pydantic AI 2.59.0 makes unknown usage visible to pricing and budget enforcement

Target catalog item: pydantic-ai-v2-57-0-approval-reviewer-mcp-apps-service-tier-pricing.

The stable release was published Oct 20 at 21:36 PT / Oct 21 04:36Z; PyPI confirms the 2.59.0 distribution at 22:00 PT / 05:00Z. The substantive change is a distinction between omitted usage and an explicit zero. Its implementation description documents an unmeasured_requests count, retained known subtotals, and UsageLimitUnavailableWarning when missing usage prevents full token/cost-limit verification. Pricing an individual response with omitted usage raises ValueError; known charges remain usable as lower bounds.

Operational qualification: the warning does not stop a run by default. A known exceeded limit still raises, and applications can promote the warning to an error when they require complete accounting. Streaming omissions are determined at completed-response boundaries. This is material Progress on the existing pricing card, not a new card for a version number. The same release adds provider-neutral error categories and an option to make selected categories non-retryable under Temporal, making retry policy easier to analyze consistently. BQAA action: preserve measured-zero, unavailable, and partially measured states through aggregation; expose budget-verification status alongside spend.

NEW 1 — Good Judgment, Bad Advice: Decision-Model Controllers Raise Agent Cost Without Improving Success — arXiv:2610.24649

Carry-forward: v1 submitted Oct 17, before this window; previously uncarded. The pack's late discovery does not make this a Tuesday-announcement paper. Full PDF read: all 8 pages, including methods, all results, limitations, references and Appendices A–D. PDF.

The study fixes one Sonnet 5.5 terminal-agent loop and varies its controller: none, deterministic rules, a small LLM, Jev 1.13, or a placebo sampled from development-phase choice frequencies. Deterministic trigger points and guardrails stay fixed. There are 21 held-out Terminal-Bench 2.1 tasks × five arms × three trials = 315 trials (§3). This design is unusually relevant to deciding whether a controller is worth invoking.

Table 1: native and Jev arms both pass 73.0% of trials; their arithmetic mean costs per attempt are $0.177 and $0.206, and cost per solved task is $0.243 and $0.283. Table 2's paired geometric-mean comparison gives a Jev/native cost ratio of 1.19, 95% CI 1.02–1.36. Jev is cheaper than the small-LLM controller (ratio 0.81), but more expensive than the placebo (1.22). These ratios use a different estimator from simply dividing the Table 1 arithmetic means.

The diagnostic result is that finish-request ranking can look good while the resulting policy is poorly calibrated: AUROC 0.78, but mean predicted completion probability 0.50 where 87% of the corresponding trials pass (§4.3, Table 3). Controller calls themselves account for only 0.11% of spend (§4.4); downstream behavior is the expensive part. No arm chooses abandonment. The observed equal pass rates do not establish equivalent success: the paired pass-rate interval remains wide, approximately −11.1 to +12.7 percentage points (Table 2).

Limits and BQAA read-through: one agent loop, a small and mostly easy task set, unpinned hosted providers, and no completed open-weight replication. Costs cover model calls, not sandbox operations. Outcome labels are not counterfactual labels for each controller action. Treat this as evidence to run controlled intervention tests, not as a fleet-wide verdict on a decision-model vendor. Log the recommended action, action actually taken, confidence, trigger, subsequent work and final verified outcome; compare with both no-controller and cheap-rule/placebo controls.

NEW 2 — DecisionPivot: A Diagnostic Benchmark for Decision Models in LLM Systems — arXiv:2610.28522

Fresh Tuesday-announcement batch; v1 Oct 20. Full PDF read: all 41 pages, including the full benchmark/protocol specification, results, limitations, references and Appendices A–F. PDF.

DecisionPivot evaluates 40 decision models and 14 general models on 48 tasks / 40,033 items: 7,800 generated items across 13 controlled task families, plus 32,233 items from 35 public test sets (§§3–4). Its useful distinction is between answering an item correctly, answering every member of a controlled contrast correctly, ordering probabilities correctly, and assigning usable confidence.

On 2,400 binary contrast pairs, Perplexity Decider orders the positive member above the negative in 92.3% of pairs, but answers both correctly in 74.1% (§4.1, Table D6). A system can rank two alternatives correctly while placing both on the wrong side of its decision threshold. These binary-pair diagnostics are not the same population as the all-13-family headline group score.

Table D1 provides a second practical example. Kev 27B and JEV-27B have similar item accuracy (70.6% versus 70.3%), yet at confidence ≥0.90 their accuracies are 93.8% on 34.1% of items and 88.9% on 47.4%, respectively. Both reliability and coverage must accompany a confidence threshold. The benchmark's “automation at 5%” metric is retrospective, label-selected coverage with family-specific cutoffs (§3.6, Eq. 2). It is not a prospective guarantee of a 5% production error rate; deployment thresholds require independent calibration.

Limits and BQAA read-through: the benchmark authors also develop models in the comparison; generated written cases are not live agent interventions. Model-specific protocols, missing outputs and input limits affect comparisons; disclosed overlap removal does not establish that every other model is contamination-free. Use the diagnostic design before using the leaderboard: retain contrast-group IDs, task family, confidence readout, threshold/version and abstention status. Evaluate these against both held-out correctness and downstream action outcomes. This complements Good Judgment: calibrated classification and beneficial intervention are separate tests.

NEW 3 — Cache Engineering for Agents — arXiv:2610.28577

Fresh Tuesday-announcement batch; v1 Oct 20. Full PDF read: all 21 pages, including cache semantics, serving implementation, benchmark/replay methods, results, limitations, references and the complete appendix. PDF.

The proposed Agentic KV Cache (AKV) lets a policy drop selected cache segments, reposition retained keys, and append new context while keeping the textual trajectory. Consequently, two requests with the same retained text and positions can have different cache states because their operation histories differ (§3.4, Figure 5). The serving implementation tracks that history for cache reuse, eviction and restoration (§4). This exposes a concrete observability requirement: a rendered conversation is insufficient to reconstruct the computation that produced an answer.

Against threshold-triggered summarization, the authors report 1.7 points higher average benchmark score and 27.7% lower modeled cost across three models and four benchmarks (Table 1, §5.2). Results are not uniformly better: GPT-OSS-120B on SWE-bench Verified rises from 40.0 to 43.2, while MiniMax-M2.7 moves from 75.2 to 74.4. The cost accounting is explicitly API-equivalent estimation using fixed token prices, including agent and summarization calls and a charge for repositioned tokens (§5.1, Appendix A.3/Table 6). It is not measured customer invoice savings.

The serving result compares peak operating points: 369 versus 313 output tokens/s and 28.49 versus 43.16 seconds P95 request latency (Table 2; AKV concurrency 14 versus standard concurrency 12). Appendix A.1.4 bounds that result: replay of the 80 longest-context SWE trajectories, method-specific recorded trajectories, replayed tool responses and fixed recorded output budgets, on eight A800 80-GB GPUs. This is a controlled serving experiment, not an online production A/B test.

BQAA implication: record logical context size, active KV size, cache-operation history/version, hits/misses and re-prefill work separately from billed input/output tokens. The paper requires serving-engine support; it is not a drop-in hosted API option. Dropping KV entries also does not prove privacy erasure: retained states can encode information from earlier context. Six-week value lies in the event schema and replay diagnostics before any commitment to a custom cache server.

NEW 4 — TraceLite: Efficient Trace Reduction for SWE Agents via Optical Compression and Redundancy Removal — arXiv:2610.28612

Fresh Tuesday-announcement batch; v1 Oct 20. Full PDF read: all 20 pages, including preliminary studies, method, main experiments, ablations, generalization, threats to validity and references. PDF.

TraceLite keeps three recent observations as text, renders older observations into compressed images, removes repeated lines, and clears pre-edit observation payloads while retaining historical tool calls (§3). It changes the agent's working context. The paper does not establish a policy for deleting an organization's archival audit traces.

On 400 held-out SWE-bench Verified tasks with GPT-5.6-Luna, Table 4 reports 313/400 solved versus 312/400 for full text. Mean cumulative input tokens fall 33.05%, from 289,507 to 193,827, and normalized modeled API cost falls 21.30%, from 1.0000 to 0.7870. But steps rise from 14.65 to 17.52 and output tokens from 7,248 to 7,621. The success comparison has McNemar p=1.000: no detected difference, not proof of equivalent task performance.

The reported 0.438 seconds per observation is compression processing overhead (Table 5), not an end-to-end task-latency reduction. A 50-task DeepSWE experiment preserves 30/50 solved at normalized cost 0.5569 (Table 10), but is a small generalization check. The preliminary all-image variant also matters: on 50 tasks, converting every observation to images at 4× compression drops solves from 45 to 29 (Table 3). Preserving recent precise evidence is central to the method.

Limits and BQAA read-through: one agent framework, limited model/task generalization, potential benchmark contamination, and verifier coverage limits (§6). The §4.2 cost calculation uses agent and auxiliary-model token charges; this brief does not infer customer-specific cache pricing or invoice savings from it. Pre-edit payload removal is a heuristic, not proof that every discarded fact is irrelevant later. Instrument transformation provenance, image/text token accounting, steps, tool retries, cost and verified success together. Keep the working-context transformation linked to the original trace so later failure analysis can recover what was removed.
