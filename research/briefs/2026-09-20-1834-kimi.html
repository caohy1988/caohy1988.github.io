1. NEW rank: BI-Agent and BI-Bench: Towards Automating End-to-End Business Intelligence — 100 (question, ground-truth-table) pairs harvested from 3K+ real Power BI projects; frontier LLMs score <50%, NL2SQL leaderboard SOTA (>70% on BIRD) collapses to 6.0–17.3%, and three specialized data-management tools (transform/join/search) lift every one of 10 LLMs (avg +14pts SQL, significant in 19/20 cells) — the strongest evidence yet that end-to-end BI is a different, harder problem than NL2SQL

Chuxuan Hu (UIUC), Yeye He + Surajit Chaudhuri (Microsoft Research), Penny Zhou + Wee Hyong Tok (Microsoft), Daniel Kang (UIUC). v1 2026-09-16; 7 sections, 5 tables, ~90 refs. Code/data: github.com/Hu-Chuxuan/bi-agent. Full text read incl. Tables 3–5 and Figs 8/10. (PDF)

  • Method: crawl 3K+ real .pbix files; programmatically extract tables, user transforms, joins, dashboards; hand-curate 100 (question, result-table) pairs from real dashboard visualizations (~400+ person-hours, multiple valid ground truths per case). BI-Agent = LLM orchestrator with generic code execution (SQL/Python) plus three specialized tools: transform (table-reshaping prediction), join (snowflake-aware join prediction over full-table statistics), search (LLM table selection). Post-training arm: 7,985 synthesized queries from held-out projects, SFT on execution-verified GPT-4o trajectories, GRPO with verifiable table-match reward. Leakage controlled by project-level dedup + embedding-similarity analysis (0.28% borderline).
  • Numbers (paper's own, Tables 3–5): tools lift all 10 LLMs — avg +14pts SQL / +11pts Python, significant in 19/20 model×language cells (o4-mini 48.2→61.9; GPT-OSS-120B 5.3→45.3, the abstract's "+40"). Post-trained Qwen3-8B-RL-Tool reaches 35.0% (+29.8 over base) at $0.19/bench vs >$10 for frontier APIs (<$200 total training). SOTA NL2SQL collapses: top-4 BIRD open-source models 6.0–17.3%; best Spider 2.0-lite agents 23.8–26.3%. Tool gains concentrate on complex tasks (57.1% relative gain on >10-table tasks); tools cut transform errors 54% and table-selection errors 41%; "missing information" is the top residual error. Statistics are unusually careful: paired t-tests, bootstrap CIs, Benjamini-Hochberg FDR, project-level robustness re-check.

Mandatory qualifications (adjudication): n=100 test cases across 82 projects (small, Power-BI-only, English-only — authors defend vs peer benchmarks but it stands); "8B matches frontier" holds only against frontier without tools (frontier-with-tools 61.9% vs best 8B 35.0%); part of the SQL tool-gain is a language artifact (SQL lacks native reshape operators — their own footnote); error taxonomy is LLM-classified; the "20.5× generalization" claim is on a 122-query subset with near-zero baselines; single post-training base model, single SFT teacher.

TL;DR for BQAA: (1) This is the most on-mission Academy card of the window — an end-to-end BI-agent benchmark from real warehouse artifacts, with verifiable table-match rewards and a trace-based error taxonomy (transform/join/selection/filter/aggregation/temporal) that maps directly onto how BQAA could classify agent failures from telemetry. (2) Specialized semantic tools beat raw reasoning over big schemas — search/join/transform primitives cut interaction rounds (8.4→6.6) and output tokens (−16%); this is quantitative support for BQAA's semantic-layer thesis from an independent lab. (3) The NL2SQL-SOTA collapse (6–26%) is the number to quote when someone cites BIRD leaderboard scores as evidence of readiness for end-to-end analytics. (4) The cost/quality datapoint (<$200 training, ~50× cheaper inference, 35% vs 61.9%) is the honest version of "small post-trained model + tools": real savings, real gap.

2. NEW rank: Efficient Benchmarking in Production: A Study of an Evolving LLM Agent — 574 runs over 52 days of a production analytics agent (tens of thousands MAU): difficulty-stratified fixed subsets + gp-IRT scoring reproduce the full 519-question benchmark at 38.5% of the cost with ~1.4pp error, stable from a single day of calibration history, and transfer to 5 other agent families without recalibration — and the team shipped the simple method, not the fancy adaptive one

Yining She (work done at Meta), Lei Lin (Meta). v1 2026-09-18; ~10pp + appendix (12 tables). No artifact release (disclosed). Full text read incl. Tables 1–12. Correction to pack/abs emphasis: the abstract's "1.03pp MAE at 200 questions" is the multidimensional-2PL appendix variant; the main-text deployed comparison is Rasch adaptive 1.37pp / fixed-subset gp-IRT 1.40pp at k=200 — best-variant framing, not an inconsistency. (PDF)

  • Method: recurring evaluation of one evolving production NL-to-data analytics agent; 519 human-curated analyst questions, binary automated grader, full run ≈ 3 hours. Compares four partial-evaluation strategies (uniform random, historical-outcome caching, difficulty-stratified fixed subsets with gp-IRT estimation, IRT adaptive testing) under a proper temporal protocol (Day 1–28 calibration, Day 29–52 held-out; 287/287 runs — no random-split leakage), then transfers the fixed subset to 5 other anonymized agent families (299 runs) and sweeps calibration-window size.
  • Numbers (Tables 1–12): k=200 (38.5% of cost): fixed-subset gp-IRT 1.40pp MAE vs random 1.79 (k=300: 0.99 vs 1.18; adaptive 0.79); ranking fidelity 0.986 Spearman at k=300. Caching is dominated at matched cost (1.54pp vs 0.60 adaptive) though it preserves rankings (0.996 Spearman). Transfer without recalibration: gp-IRT beats random at 11/12 budgets (1.47 vs 1.86 at k=200). 1-day calibration window (14 runs) still yields 1.41pp vs 1.40 for 4 weeks. The team deployed fixed subsets over adaptive IRT for orchestration simplicity and per-question comparability.

Mandatory qualifications (authors' own + adjudication): one organization, one production benchmark; agent/model family undisclosed and the 5 transfer families anonymized (transfer claims unprobeable); single automated binary grader with no grader-error analysis; adaptive-testing gains exist only in simulation over recorded runs (they shipped the simpler thing); no regression-detection / release-gate analysis (MAE and rank correlation, not missed regressions or false alarms — the metric an agent-analytics guardrail actually needs); no artifacts.

TL;DR for BQAA: (1) A cost/latency playbook for recurring eval of an analytics agent — BQAA's exact shape, with production data behind it: stratified fixed subsets + gp-IRT at ~38% cost, ~1.4pp error, 1 day of calibration, cross-framework transfer. Directly reusable for BQAA eval-gate design. (2) The deployment decision is the lesson: fancy adaptive IRT won on paper and lost on operations — per-question comparability and orchestration simplicity are first-class requirements for eval infrastructure. (3) The open niche they punt on — regression detection and false-alarm semantics for release gates — is precisely where BQAA telemetry-driven guardrails could contribute.

3. NEW rank: What Stops a Small Language Model From Driving a Database Agent — an 11-day, 39-model field study (8,199 runs / 110,711 ledger events / 14,008 refused tool calls) of a read-only database agent: 75.7% of agent-mode losses came from runs that invoked ≥1 tool — engagement, not raw capability — and five server-only fixes (no model/prompt changes) moved 6 models by 6–21 cells of 30

Cevheri Bozoğlan, Yusuf Gündoğdu, Abdullah Kaya, Koray Şirin (LibreDB Studio contributors). v1 2026-09-18; ~10pp. Dataset + scorer + verifier released (HF doi:10.57967/hf/10485); app MIT-licensed. Full text read incl. Tables 2/4/6/8 and §8. Correction to pack/abs claim: "transport (36.2%) is the largest failure class" does not survive the authors' own model-clustered resampling (largest in only 74.5% of resamples, CI [22.0, 49.1]); what survives (99.7%) is the engagement claim — card accordingly. (PDF)

  • Method: field study, not a benchmark — 39 locally-served open-weight models (1.7B–35B) + 1 hosted control driving LibreDB Studio's agent mode over 6 task surfaces; unit = "cell" (model × surface, locked by 5 consecutive passes; strict "row" scoring — pooled scorers shown to overstate by up to 9 cells). Four-class failure taxonomy from a structured run ledger (clock / capability / verification / transport). Anti-hallucination tool contract: every report claim must cite a real run artifact (correlation id / schema fingerprint), server-checked. Temporary capture of refused-call arguments (production ledger omits them) diagnosed transport failures → 5 server defects → 5 server-only fixes.
  • Numbers (Tables 2–8): 8,199 runs, 60.8% answered, 14,008 refused tool calls — compose_report alone = 9,707 refusals (69.3%): the delivery tool is the chokepoint. Taxonomy (2,100 agent-mode losses): transport 36.2% / clock 25.8% / verification 20.7% / capability 17.3% (under alternative ordering capability rises to 24.3% — both reported). 75.7% of losses involved ≥1 tool call (robust: 99.7% of clustered resamples). Five server fixes moved 6 models by 6–21/30 cells (sign test p=0.031). Documented confounds: a 12B model at full 262k context held 51GB on a 64GB box; a 3B model's 0/5 cell read 5/5 once context was capped — all headline figures are lower bounds with unknown per-model bias.

Mandatory qualifications (adjudication; the paper is unusually self-critical): one machine, one small fixed schema, one agent implementation — the taxonomy is a property of this tool contract; uncontrolled field observations (no randomization, run counts 3–629 per model allocated by operational interest, corpus spans 5 code checkouts — "nothing here identifies a cause," their words); all §5 results predate the context-cap fix; intervention table is not an ablation (selection-on-open-cells, regression-to-mean); authors built the software under test (self-evaluation, mitigated by released verifier/dataset); models ≤35B, single hosted control.

TL;DR for BQAA: (1) The run ledger is a working blueprint for agent analytics instrumentation — tool invoked/completed/refused, verdicts, correlation-id evidence citations — including the trap that status: succeeded ≠ task success (2,204 model-stopped runs, 1,405 unanswered). (2) Most small-model agent losses are interface/transport, fixable server-side — before blaming the model, capture refused-call arguments and audit the tool contract (their compose_report contradiction: presentation required on one tool, forbidden on a sibling). (3) Serving-context confounds (swap pressure, context caps) can zero out a model's score — any BQAA benchmarking of local models needs per-run environment telemetry alongside outcome telemetry.

4. NEW rank: When Better Turns Do Not Make Better Agents: Diagnosing the Gap Between Next-Turn Metrics and Workflow Success — SFT lifts gold-history next-turn success +19–25pts for every model and transfers almost nowhere: best strict workflow completion is 10.4% (8/84), holistic success is 0% pre- and post-SFT, and one model family's tool-call accuracy regresses to zero under training its turn metrics call an improvement

Md Tahmid Rahman Laskar, Xue-Yong Fu, Gundeep Singh, et al. (Dialpad Inc.). v1 2026-09-18; ~8–10pp incl. appendices. No code/data (proprietary; sanitized prompt templates only). Full text read incl. §4 tables. (PDF)

  • Method: 130 proprietary customer-support workflows → 1,800 GPT-5-simulated multi-turn conversations → dual-judge filter (Claude-4.5-Opus + Gemini-2.5-Pro) → 1,027 conversations; next-action SFT (5,834 train examples) on Qwen3-4B/14B and Gemma3-4B/12B. Five-tier eval protocol on the same workflows and models, varying only the metric: ROUGE-1 text similarity; gold-history turn success (LLM judge); strict tool correctness (deterministic exact function + normalized-arg match); closed-loop replay over 84 held-out conversations with deterministic user simulator; holistic workflow judgment.
  • Numbers (Table 1 / §4): text ROUGE-1 24.8→49.2; LLM-judged text-turn success 33.2%→58.4%; all-turn success 28.5%→47.7%. Tool use lags badly: tool-turn success 17.8%→23.7%; exact call accuracy 3.5%→8.5%; argument F1 6.7%→14.3%. Gemma3-4B regresses (exact call 3.0%→0.0%) — the SFT gains are Qwen-only. End-to-end: zero workflows completed pre-SFT; post-SFT best = Qwen3-14B 8/84 (10.4%); both Gemmas 0; holistic judgment: 0% for all models, both pre- and post-SFT.

Mandatory qualifications (authors' own + adjudication): two small model families (≤14B) in one proprietary domain — the title's generality overreaches the evidence; all data is synthetic (GPT-5-generated, LLM-filtered) — "workflow success" is success against a synthetic distribution; small eval n (84 conversations; the 10.4% = 8 conversations); LLM-judge metrics with no judge-agreement validation; exact-match replay can reject valid alternative paths (the 0% holistic result partly reflects protocol strictness); nothing released — not reproducible.

TL;DR for BQAA: (1) Quantitative ammunition for trajectory-level over turn-level analytics: per-turn telemetry (next-action accuracy, judged turn success) systematically overstated end-to-end agent health by ~5× here — the core argument for session/workflow-level metrics in BQAA dashboards. (2) The five-tier protocol is a copyable decomposition for BigQuery-based agent trace evaluation: text quality / local action correctness / deterministic tool-execution match / closed-loop replay / holistic judgment — with disaggregated per-capability telemetry so tool-execution regressions can't hide inside aggregate turn metrics (the Gemma case). (3) Cite with the scope flags: synthetic data, ≤14B models, n=84.

5. NEW rank: DENSE: Distilling Agent Trajectories into Evidence-Grounded Shortcut Trees for Self-Refinement — given only a finished trajectory (no rewards, no verifier access, no gold answers), an LLM-built subtask tree of compressed "shortcuts" lets a fresh retry with fully reset environment and context gain +7.1 to +15.6pp strict pass across 4 recipient models while cutting observed tokens 19–44% — and the REFIT paired-rerun protocol is itself a reusable eval design

Fan Yu (Fudan U + Meituan Longcat), Dongyu Ru, Yizhu Liu, Yifan Yang, Xuezhi Cao, Xunliang Cai (Meituan), Yixin Cao (Fudan). v1 2026-09-18; long paper incl. 6 appendix sections; anonymous supplementary package only (no public repo; raw trajectories excluded) — appears under double-blind review. Full text read incl. Tables 2–6 and App F. Correction to pack framing: DENSE is best among non-privileged feedback — privileged Verifier Feedback still beats it on GPT-5.5 and Kimi K2.6; and under the all-3-attempts consistency metric, raw-trajectory replay beats DENSE on DeepSeek. (PDF)

  • Method: "post-hoc outcome blindness" — from task + trajectory alone, distill feedback for a retry at the same task; feedback is the only information channel (REFIT protocol: all methods share the same run0, source-paired). DENSE builds a nested subtask tree over the action/observation stream; sibling attempts compress into shortcuts (failed approaches + working path + evidence + unresolved requirements); cross-level reconciliation closes issues only with concrete recovery evidence; deterministic checks constrain source coverage, LLM does only semantic judgments. Eval: Terminal-Bench 2.1 (89 containerized tasks), 4 recipient models, 3 reps/task, strict pass = verifier reward exactly 1.
  • Numbers (Tables 2–4): DENSE vs shared-run0 baseline: MiniMax-M2.7 52.4 vs 44.6 (+7.9pp); DeepSeek V4 Pro 67.1 vs 51.4 (+15.6pp); GPT-5.5 75.7 vs 68.5 (+7.1pp); Kimi K2.6 50.9 vs 39.3 (+11.6pp); tokens −19.0/−20.0/−43.6/−23.7%. GPT-5.5 cost: $1.15 vs $1.81 baseline (−36.6%), feedback ≈$0.14/task. Ablations modest (−3.0–3.4pp).

Mandatory qualifications (authors' own + adjudication): single benchmark; same-task retry is a narrow slice of online improvement (no cross-task transfer shown); 3 reps/task → pass cells quantize to 0/33/67/100% and ~7pp ≈ ~18 task-flips with no significance tests; feedback generator fixed to Gemini 3 Flash Preview (generator-family sensitivity untested); subtask boundary detection is hand-tuned prompt engineering; no public code and raw traces withheld → full replication impossible; ethics note concedes feedback can preserve errors/unsafe actions from source traces.

TL;DR for BQAA: (1) A concrete method for mining agent traces into structured, evidence-linked artifacts — subtask trees, shortcuts, unresolved-requirement ledgers — i.e., a trace semantic-layer + compression pipeline over telemetry of exactly the kind BQAA stores. (2) The REFIT protocol is the stealable half: reset-environment paired reruns to measure whether a trace-derived insight actually changes the next run — a general eval design for any analytics/attribution output ("does this finding do anything?"). (3) The "historical success ≠ current state" pitfall (a recipient claiming a non-recreated artifact was done) is a guardrail lesson for trace-driven feedback: verify state, don't inherit it.

6. NEW rank: APort Vault: Benchmarking AI Agent Payment Authorization with the Open Agent Passport — 4,371 human-written CTF attacks replayed across 14 models from 8 labs (225,964 evaluations): models alone execute 140 unpermitted transfers, a deterministic pre-action authorization layer executes 0 (bounded ≤0.38%/session) while still passing 25,370 legitimate payments — and the LLM-judge audit layer misses up to 35.6% of deterministically confirmed outcomes

Uchi Uchibeke (APort Technologies — solo author, and the company sells the layer under test; disclosed). v1 2026-09-18; cs.CR/cs.AI; ~15pp. Full dataset + scoring code + pre-registration released (HF aporthq/vault-benchmark-v1, frozen snapshot). Full text read incl. Tables 2–5 and §4.4–4.10. (PDF)

  • Method: 4,371 human-authored attacks from a public capture-the-flag against a live simulated agentic bank ($6,500 prize, 1,128 sessions). Replay grid: 14 models × 5 policy levels × 2 replay tracks × 2 architectures (model alone vs behind a deterministic OAP policy engine in the tool-dispatch path); the only manipulated variable is the authorization check (same model/prompt/decode/tool schema). Headline metric is deterministic (executed tool calls vs permissions — no LLM judge needed); a 2-judge panel runs only as an audited audit layer. Session-clustered percentile bootstrap, rule-of-three zero bounds, BH-FDR, pre-registered.
  • Numbers (Tables 2–5): unpermitted transfers 140/76,842 model-alone vs 0/69,297 behind the layer (matched triples: 105 vs 0 on 68,970); the zero is honestly bounded (per-session upper bound 0.38%) and is not blanket refusal — 25,370 successful payments executed, only 187/25,640 transfer calls policy-denied. Request rates barely change with the layer (+0.084pp paired) — enforcement, not suppression. Failures concentrate: 140 failures from just 24/790 sessions (one session = 47.9%); no attack defeated >5 of 14 models.
