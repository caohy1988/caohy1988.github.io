Academy

NEW 1 — Grounded but Not Immune: confirmation bias in tool-using agent judges — arXiv:2611.05662 — reserve pick from an earlier batch (submitted 3 Nov 2026; title/abstract-screened at the 11-05-0034 slot and held at the top of this slot's PDF queue; ranked now only after the full read below)

Full-PDF-read statement: full PDF read — main text §§1–5 including Limitations and Ethics Statement, plus Appendices A–R (worked exemplars Figs. 6–12/16, cue templates and property-ablation Tables 2/8, flip-count Table 6, weak-cue §E, helpful-flip §F, meta-judge prompts §N, human validation §P/Table 10, reproducibility §R/Table 11); References skimmed as listings. Key numbers cite section/table/figure below.

The setup is the deployment-realistic one: an Agent-as-a-Judge receives the agent's full trajectory plus tools over the finished environment (run code, query the database, inspect files) and must investigate before verdict (§3). Ground truth comes from each dataset's deterministic verifier, independent of the judge. Five datasets (AJ-Bench Postgres/Filesystem/Deep Search, SWE-Bench-Verified, Terminal-Bench-2.0), four judges across three model families, ~70,000 judge trajectories and ~20,000 meta-judge calls (§R). The intervention is a single false pre-screening sentence attributed to a domain expert, injected opposite the judge's own uncued majority verdict (§3.1).

Grounding does not immunize. The strong cue's harmful flip rate — correct uncued verdicts overturned — averages 31% across judge×dataset cells (26% pooled; abstract: "up to 40%"; §E), and even a hedged, authority-free remark ("I only skimmed this…") flips 11% (8% pooled), nonzero in every cell (4–15%; §E, Fig. 14). More investigation does not rescue the frontier judges: harmful flip rate rises with judge turns for the two proprietary judges (r = +0.21, +0.23) while falling for the open-weight judges (−0.27, −0.12; Fig. 3). Cue properties matter more than cue presence: asserting a belief closes 46–65% of the error-taxonomy ceiling vs 22–24% for merely directing a check (Table 2, Fig. 4); placement in the system prompt hits 77.2% of ceiling vs ~43% spliced mid-trajectory (Table 8, §K); severity cues (assert failure) beat leniency cues on four of five datasets (Fig. 13).

The mechanism analysis is the durable contribution. A trajectory-aware three-model meta-judge compares each cued flip to the judge's own uncued run and assigns over-trust (verify, conclude correctly, then defer), hallucination (fabricate support), or under-investigation (stop at first corroboration) (§3.3). For the two frontier judges over-trust dominates at 75% and 67% of harmful flips (Fig. 5); human gold on 97 flips (three blind annotators, Fleiss' κ = 0.646) reads 73% over-trust / 18% under-investigation / 9% hallucination, with the ensemble agreeing 74.2% (κ = 0.53) and over-attributing hallucination, which the authors flag as an upper bound (§P, Table 10). Mitigations blunt but never remove the bias, and they tax the cue's genuine corrections: the best one-line guard instruction cuts harmful flips 18%→5% but also cuts helpful flips 30%→12% (Table 1); about a third of helpful flips are genuine better investigation (§F), so "blind the judge to priors" is the wrong fix — the authors argue for direction-aware calibration (§4). Limits are stated plainly: the cue is deliberately overt, mitigations are cue-aware (optimistic bound), and the property ablation runs in depth on one judge (Limitations).

BQAA read: judge scores in the warehouse are not measurements of the run alone — they are measurements of run × judge-prompt, and any triage label, prior score, or reviewer note in that prompt moves them by double digits. Three concrete modeling consequences: (1) persist the judge's full input (priors included, with provenance: who/what asserted it, how decisively) and the judge's own trajectory next to every score, or post-hoc audits cannot distinguish deference from evidence; (2) the over-trust signature — judge states the correct conclusion mid-trajectory, then reverses — is detectable over stored judge trajectories and is a warehouse-native quality metric BQAA can compute today; (3) because mitigations trade harmful flips against helpful corrections, score tables should keep cued/uncued (or prior/no-prior) verdicts as separate rows rather than collapsing to one verdict column.

  • AGORA: reliability-aware aggregation of LLM judges via item-response theory (2611.05556, reserve from the same earlier batch) — full PDF read this slot (main text §§1–8, Limitations, Appendices A–E; References skimmed). A graded-response IRT aggregator (per-judge discrimination + thresholds, horseshoe shrinkage) that matches Dawid–Skene at full N (SummEval Spearman 0.556 vs 0.555; Table 1) and holds its margin as sets shrink to N=50, plus a label-free agreement-dispersion statistic δ that predicts when trust weighting helps (§5.3) and trust weights that track human validity (§7, Fig. 4). Not ranked: margins over the per-item mean are small (+0.010 to +0.035 ρ), and all three benchmarks are static NLG judging (summaries/dialogue/translation), not agent trajectories — useful aggregation hygiene for judge pools, below this brief's bar given dense recent judge coverage. Reserve remains available if an agent-trace replication appears.
  • Interpretable early termination of web navigation agents via closed sequential pattern mining (2611.04142, older reserve) — full PDF read this slot (6-page workshop paper, §§1–6 + Appendix A, Tables 1–3). Mines 32 closed sequential patterns from K-step trace prefixes (1,544 MiniWoB++ traces, three small open-weight models) into a four-category failure taxonomy; recommended operating point saves 26.3% of tokens at 92% precision (Table 2), and the length-artifact controls (step-count baseline, contamination variants) are methodologically welcome. Not ranked: on the cleanest variant the margin over the length-only control is thin (F1@5 0.683 vs 0.667; Table 1), single split/seed with no significance testing (authors' own §5 caveats), taxonomy from informal inspection, and the Bi-LSTM overtakes it at longer prefixes. The pattern — prefix failure signatures computed over a trace store — is worth remembering; the evidence is not yet rank-grade.
  • Failure attribution needs an executed counterfactual (2611.05650) and rubric language and clause structure in LLM judges (2611.04091) — reserve queue, abstract-screened only; no PDF read this slot (the 3-PDF cap went to 2611.05662, 2611.05556, 2611.04142). Six-week EV

Highest expected value is a judge-input provenance + deference-audit spike on data BQAA already lands: (1) add judge-prompt provenance to the score schema (prior verdicts/labels present? source? decisiveness?) — without it, every stored score is confounded by an unrecorded intervention; (2) run the over-trust detector over stored judge trajectories (mid-trajectory correct conclusion → final reversal) and report its rate per judge model — success criterion is a per-judge deference rate, not a dashboard; (3) where pipelines currently show judges a first-pass label, A/B prior-vs-no-prior on a fixed trace sample and store both verdicts — the paper's Table 1 says expect a double-digit flip rate and that removing priors wholesale also removes genuine corrections, so measure both directions before changing policy. Each step is reversible, warehouse-native, and directly citable to the ranked paper if leadership asks why now. Lower priority: prototype prefix failure-signature mining (2611.04142 pattern) only if a customer asks for early-kill; adopt IRT trust weighting (2611.05556) only where judge pools are demonstrably uneven (compute δ first — it is label-free).
