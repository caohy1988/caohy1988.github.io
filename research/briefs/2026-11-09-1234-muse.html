Academy

NEW 1 — Knowing When to Intervene: Causal Evaluation of AI Agents — https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.09792

Full PDF read (pdftotext, every part including Sections 1–4, Figures 1–9, Table 1, Limitations, and Appendices A–C with Tables 2–9 and Figures 10–14). This is the reserve candidate from 2026-11-09-0034, abstract-screened there and full-PDF-read this slot; the catalog's only prior mention is that brief's scout line, not a card.

The Stanford team treats "should we review this tool call?" as a dynamic causal question that static benchmarks cannot answer. Their design (Section 2, Figure 1) randomizes intervention vs control at every eligible decision inside each trajectory (uniform Bernoulli, e=1/2), records the frozen proposal and observed history, and lets the agent continue — black-box, no Markov assumption, no counterfactual replay. From that one sequentially randomized dataset they estimate three estimands (Section 2.3, Figure 2): the blip effect of intervening now with future assignments still randomized (cross-fitted AIPW with known propensities, Appendix A.2), the blip with no further interventions, and the policy gain of always reviewing a chosen group vs never reviewing (the latter two via structural nested mean models with g-estimation, Appendix A.3–A.4). Averaged over groups defined by pre-assignment history (tool family, individual tool, progress state), the blips become a "decision impact score" leaderboard for audit prioritization.

Evidence is at real scale (Table 1; Appendix B): τ-bench Retail tool-call review (2,280 randomized trajectories across 228 tasks; two small agents, a stronger reviewer) finds positive review effects concentrated in order-handling writes for one agent, while for the other the all-reads average is ~zero yet the account-details subgroup's interval sits above zero — the aggregate hides the target (Figures 3–4). The method validates itself: SNMM off-policy estimates of nine selective review policies, computed from the randomized data alone, overlap direct Monte Carlo rollouts (456 episodes per policy) on all nine, with the direct point estimate inside the SNMM interval for eight of nine (Figure 5). ToolSandbox (3,096 trajectories per agent, 33 tools, 49 groups) shows the same granularity lesson — the Time/calculations capability blip is +1.8 points [−0.2, 3.8] while timestamp construction alone is +6.9 [1.2, 12.6] (Section 3.2, Figures 6–8). ImpossibleBench (1,218 analyzed episodes) flips the sign convention to measure harm: adversarial feedback after a failed submission raises terminal rule violations by an estimated 9.4pp [2.9, 15.9] at the last-chance checkpoint vs 3.3pp [0.6, 6.1] after the first failure, and 5.7pp [2.3, 9.2] when ≥80% of tests pass vs 0.3pp [−1.7, 2.2] below 50% (Section 3.3, Figure 9) — vulnerability is a state, not a constant.

Why it matters for BQAA: this is the measurement design that turns stored trajectories into a priced review policy. It needs exactly what an agent-analytics store should keep — per-decision proposal, context features, intervention assignment with its probability, and terminal outcome — and it returns per-tool-group causal effects with intervals instead of correlational failure counts. Limits are stated plainly (Section 4): SNMM/policy-gain estimates need a correctly specified blip model (only the randomized-continuation AIPW blip is assumption-light); agents are small models on possibly saturating benchmarks; history features are hand-designed; intervals are pointwise without multiple-comparison correction (Section 3.2). Card it as a design + estimator recipe with a validation protocol, not as transferable effect sizes.
