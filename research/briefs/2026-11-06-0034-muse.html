NEW 1 — How Do AI Agents Waste Your Money? Token waste measured per-span and validated by removal — arXiv:2611.07238

Full-PDF-read statement: full PDF read — main text §§1–9 including Limitations, plus Appendix A (reasoning-effort behavior) and Appendix B (judge system prompt); References skimmed as listings. Key numbers cite section/table/figure below.

The paper separates token cost from token waste: content removable without eliminating useful work. A proxy-based tracer normalizes complete trajectories across harnesses and providers (§2.1); an LLM judge marks removable spans in reasoning, visible replies, and tool calls under four patterns — Restate, Duplicate-Fetch, Padding (together "strict waste") and Detour (reasonable when attempted, unnecessary in hindsight) (§2.2–2.3) — and waste tokens are recounted under each provider's native accounting. Six frontier models, five task sets across customer-service, coding, and general tool-use benchmarks (§3).

The measurement is validated by intervention, which is what lifts it above judge-labeling exercises. Deleting marked strict waste mid-trajectory and resuming preserves solve rates at most intervention points (Table 1; the exception is first-turn pruning on the tool-use benchmark, 75.3%→65.6%); next-action distributions shift significantly on only 3.0% of coding turns vs 25.0% when all prior reasoning is deleted (Table 2); and online pruning during execution removes 41% of reasoning on the coding benchmark with solve rate 89.0%→89.5%, and 46% on the tool-use benchmark at 65.0%→62.3%, vs 73.0%/56.0% when reasoning is deleted entirely (Table 3). Headline magnitudes: ~18% of output tokens are waste, and for over 45% of tasks one agent's wasted output alone exceeds another agent's entire successful output (§5, §9).

The structure matters more than the average. Waste varies much more across tasks/environments and harnesses than across models (Fig. 6); detours dominate the waste mix (Fig. 5); longer contexts do not reliably mean more waste — waste often causes the long context (§5.3). More reasoning effort does not simply become waste: at maximum effort the model emits 6.6× the reasoning tokens of low effort yet ~66% is still judged non-wasteful, while 81% of the extra tokens from high→max land on tasks whose outcome does not change (§6.1, §6.3, Fig. 14). Same model, same tasks, five harnesses: waste ratios and action mixes move materially, and some waste traces to concrete harness defects (wrong Python environment at shell launch; a timeout schema the runtime itself rejects) while other patterns persist across harnesses and are model×task effects (§7, Figs. 18–20). Stated limits: waste labels come from an LLM judge (detours stay judgment calls), and full-reasoning analysis is possible only for the one model that exposes its reasoning channel (Limitations).

BQAA read: this is a warehouse-native process metric with an intervention-backed definition. Token totals per run — the number every cost dashboard shows — cannot distinguish a long necessary run from a wasteful one; span-level waste labels joined to task, harness, and model can. Concrete modeling consequences: (1) store waste-span annotations (pattern, span offsets, judge version) as a first-class table over trajectories, split strict waste from detours — they call for different fixes (prompt/harness hygiene vs exploration policy); (2) attribute waste by task family and harness before blaming models — the paper's variance ordering says model choice is the weakest lever; (3) treat "reasoning tokens" and "visible execution tokens" as separate measures, since effort moves their waste in opposite directions (§6.2). Framework, code, and labeled trajectories are open-sourced (§1), so the labeling pipeline is directly testable on BQAA-landed traces.

NEW 2 — Hard Issue, Weak Model, or Bad Run? Trajectory failure predictors learn the task and the model, not the run — arXiv:2611.08285

Full-PDF-read statement: full PDF read — complete 5-page paper (§§I–V, Tables I–II, Figs. 1–2, including Threats to Validity); References skimmed as listings. The authors label §III "Preliminary Findings" — read the rank with that scope attached.

Trajectory-based failure predictors promise early warning: read a partial run, flag likely failure, then stop, retry, or switch models. This paper decomposes failure into three causes with different correct actions — hard issue (stop/escalate to human), weak model (switch), bad run (retry) — and tests whether predictors can tell them apart on 3,492 public coding-agent runs across seven models (plus a 23-model outcome set and a repeated-run corpus) (§II). Predictors see trajectory text only (no issue text, no model name), in 18 configurations.

They cannot. A run-blind issue-difficulty score — the fraction of outside models failing the issue — reaches 0.924 pooled AUROC while trajectory predictors reach 0.624–0.630 and do not beat prefix-length-plus-model-identity (Table I). Within an issue, model identity alone (0.724) beats the predictors (0.604–0.610), and on "upset" pairs — where the overall weaker model is the one that failed — every predictor configuration scores below chance (0.415–0.416), the signature of having learned the ability ranking rather than the run. Adding predictor scores to difficulty+ability changes AUROC by an amount statistically equivalent to zero in all 18 configurations (equivalence-tested; §III-A). The usual justification for model switching also collapses: the 10.0pp oracle gap (86.8% any-model vs 76.8% best-single) sits inside the range chance alone produces under a no-specialization reference (Fig. 2), choosing a model per issue from past runs gains +0.09pp on held-out runs, and escalation's +6.91pp comes from the second attempt, not from picking a better-suited model (Table II). Variance decomposition on repeated runs: issue difficulty 53%, run-to-run variation 42%, model×issue specialization 4.6% — bad runs are common enough for retries to pay, but 64–82% of predictor-score variance lies between issues, so alarms flag exactly the hard-issue failures retries rarely fix (§III-C). Threats are stated plainly: one benchmark and scaffold, simple text predictors (a small fine-tuned neural check behaves the same), older models in the repeated-run pilot, and non-rejection does not prove specialization absent (§IV).

BQAA read: any early-kill, retry, or routing feature built on trajectory scores must first beat run-blind baselines within task — pooled AUROC over a trace warehouse will look excellent while encoding only "hard task, weak model." The warehouse-side requirements follow directly: (1) evaluation tables for run-health models should report within-task AUROC next to pooled, against difficulty/ability/length references — all computable from stored outcomes; (2) repeated runs of the same task×model must be retained as first-class rows, because single-attempt data cannot separate a bad run from a hard task at all; (3) router/switching claims should be booked against an equal-attempts baseline (same model twice), or the "gain" is just a second sample. This extends the line opened by arxiv-2610-35971-early-failure-prediction-limits-coding-agents from "early prediction is hard" to a specific confound decomposition with tests BQAA can run on its own landed data.

NEW 3 — Benchmarks as Software: a third of Terminal-Bench 2.0 was defective, and budgets are part of the measurement — arXiv:2611.08417

Full-PDF-read statement: full PDF read — main text §§1–8 plus Appendices A–C (audit-workflow evaluation Table 1, per-task repair map Tables 2–3, agent configurations Tables 4–6, per-task pass-rate changes Table 7, failure decomposition §C.2); References skimmed as listings. The paper has no separate Limitations section; its own caveats (§4, §8) are noted where used below.

The authors name the failure mode "benchmark maintenance debt": even a benchmark that passed human review, LM-assisted auditing, and exploit red-teaming at release decayed within months — 50+ open task-defect reports — as agents found valid solutions verifiers rejected and external dependencies shifted (§1). Their remedy is a continuous-validation workflow (Fig. 2): an evidence agent assembles task files, prior execution traces, and community reports; an auditor agent labels defects under a small repair-oriented rubric; a verifier reruns the oracle solution and challenges the label; humans gate the merge (§4). Applied to Terminal-Bench 2.0's 89 tasks, 28 needed repair: instruction–verification mismatch in 57% of revised tasks, task drift 43%, environment/resource issues 29% (Fig. 3a, §5) — released as Terminal-Bench 2.1.

Repairs move conclusions, not just cosmetics. Re-evaluating 19 agents: rank order survives (Spearman ρ = 0.958) but individual scores shift by up to 12pp and leaderboard positions by up to three ranks; five verifier-defective tasks jump from ≤15% to >50% pass rates and account for ~62.7% of the total pass-rate change (§6.1, Fig. 4, Table 7). Resource budgets are the second measurement surface: over 50% of failed trials at the default budget are timeouts (up to ~68% for one harness; Fig. 3b), and extending wall-clock up to 32× closes most of a ~20pp within-vendor harness gap — i.e., much of the "harness effect" at fixed budget is speed, not capability (§6.2, Fig. 6). The workflow itself is honestly scoped: capable auditors agree on defect category at κ = 0.80, cheaper auditors triage reliably (κ = 0.55–0.77) but attribute poorly, drift detection leans on the deterministic oracle rerun, and the reported 57.1% precision / 71.4% recall is retrospective agreement with a release the workflow helped build, not independent discovery (§4, Table 1). A transfer check on a second benchmark snapshot confirmed 12 of 14 flags (86% precision) while missing defect classes needing repeated execution or domain expertise (§7).

BQAA read: evaluation artifacts stored next to traces — tasks, verifiers, golden sets, judge rubrics — are software with a decay rate, and ~30% defective within months is the measured scale for a well-reviewed benchmark. Warehouse consequences: (1) version every eval artifact (task set digest, verifier version, budget spec) and join scores to those versions, or longitudinal "model improved/regressed" queries silently mix measurement change with capability change; (2) budget fields (wall-clock, token caps, retries) belong in the score schema as measurement parameters — a 20pp harness gap that is mostly timeout policy should never be readable as a capability gap; (3) the cheap continuous check with the highest yield is the oracle rerun: re-executing reference solutions against current environments catches drift deterministically, where judge-style auditing only triages. For a team whose product is the system of record for agent evaluation, "which benchmark version, under what budget, last oracle-validated when" is core metadata, not provenance garnish.

Six-week EV

Highest expected value is a measurement-integrity pass over data BQAA already lands, sequenced by cost: (1) Run-health baseline audit (days, SQL only): for any stored failure/quality score, compute within-task discrimination against run-blind difficulty and model-identity references (2611.08285's Table I protocol). Success criterion: a per-score verdict — beats run-blind within task, or it is a difficulty proxy and gets labeled as such before anyone builds early-kill or routing on it. Requires repeated task×model runs to exist as rows; where they do not, that absence is the finding. (2) Waste-span labeling spike (1–2 weeks): run 2611.07238's open-sourced judge over a stratified sample of landed trajectories; store strict-waste vs detour spans with task/harness/model joins; validate on a small intervention subset (delete-and-resume) before trusting ratios. Success criterion: a waste table whose top harness×task cells replicate the paper's ordering (task/harness variance > model variance) — or a documented divergence. (3) Eval-artifact versioning + oracle-rerun job (2–4 weeks): add task-set digest, verifier version, and budget spec to every score row, and schedule reference-solution reruns against current environments (2611.08417's /oracle check) with drift flags. Success criterion: any historical score is reproducible to an exact artifact version, and drifted tasks surface as measurement incidents, not model regressions. All three are reversible, warehouse-native, and directly citable to this slot's ranked papers. Lower priority: closed-loop evaluation architecture (2611.07051) as a design reference when the eval-roadmap next opens; two-ledger cost attribution (2611.06989) only if a FinOps stakeholder asks for per-transaction AI cost.
