NEW 1 — Who Verifies the Graph? Misspecification attacks on causal action verification for language agents (2609.40027)

Full PDF read (10 pp.). Single author, red-teaming his own earlier verifier CIVeX (2605.09168). NeurIPS 2026 workshop "Who Verifies the Agents?".

What it does. A causal action verifier gates each state-changing tool call. It checks the action against a committed action–state graph and issues a certificate: graph, adjustment set, estimate, 95% lower bound. The paper leaves the verifier, data and estimator alone and corrupts only the graph. The benchmark is a synthetic tool-use workload: 6 workflow families × 7 seeds × 25 instances, so n = 1,050 actions per cell.

  • Attack 1, one omitted edge (§3, Fig. 1). Dropping the hidden-confounder edge from the graph:
  • Weak confounding: harmless, even profitable. Utility is +2.79 against +2.27 for the honest graph, because the honest graph pays for experiments it doesn't need.
  • At the published strength (2.5): false executions are 15.3% (seed range 13.3–17.3%), 91% of executions are harmful, and utility falls to +0.35.
  • From strength 3.0 up: 41–47% false executions and negative utility.
  • The certificate looks identical on both sides of the cliff.
  • Attacks 2 and 3 (§4, Table 1).
  • Collider admitted as a confounder: fails safe. Correct executions drop from 52.9% to 7.9%, with no false executions.
  • One reversed arrow on a real mediator: total inversion. 48.9% false executions, 0% correct, 100% of beneficial actions lost, utility −1.36.
  • Defense: check each execution against a small randomized experiment (§5, Table 2).
  • Detection: caught every harmful execution generated, with 2 false alarms in 555 executions on a true graph. Zero observed false executions in 1,050 actions bounds the true rate below about 0.29% (rule of three).
  • Response matters more than detection: at harmless strength 1.0, refusing on every flag drops utility to +1.05. Falling back to the randomized result keeps +2.76.
  • Experiment availability decides whether zero is reachable: only 70.6% of actions have a safe experiment. Letting the untestable ones through brings false executions back to 4.3%.
  • The fallback can inherit a different error (Table 3): re-deciding with the committed graph's adjustment set reproduces the mediator error at 34.6–48.9%. Only a graph-free difference in means fixes it.
  • Main result: the costs are lopsided (§6, Table 4). Spot-checking executions removes false executions but leaves 97.1% of beneficial actions rejected.
  • Why: 539 of 555 beneficial actions are rejected at triage, and those rejections never get checked.
  • Auditing rejections works but is expensive: restoring safety cost 127 experiments; restoring the lost value cost 614 more. Auditing every rejection reproduces the honest graph exactly, at the same 741 experiments.
  • Limitations (§8):
  • synthetic benchmark, with confounding strength swept only for the omitted edge;
  • no real tool-agent benchmark;
  • attestation assumes a cheap randomized sample, which irreversible actions don't have.

BQAA read. This paper is about what a decision log has to contain to be auditable.

  • Log rejections like executions. A REJECT that carries its certificate can be audited later. A REJECT logged as "not executed" cannot be.
  • Measure wrongful inaction separately. A dashboard of false executions alone will show a clean record while the verifier quietly does nothing ("A deployment that monitors false executions alone will see a verifier with a clean record while it quietly does nothing", §6).
  • Version the graph. The graph or policy behind each verdict should be a versioned attribute on the decision span, because a single edited edge flips outcomes while every certificate validates.

NEW 2 — Auditable Long-Term Memory: a deterministic retrieval chain measured at 479/475 of 500 on LongMemEval-S (2609.38021)

Full PDF read (14 pp.). Single author, a technical report on the retrieval core of the Archivist memory product. Evidence released at github.com/cjchanh/longmemeval-evidence.

What it does. Stages 1–4 are deterministic code, held byte-identical across runs by verification gates:

  1. hybrid retrieval;
  2. cross-encoder rerank;
  3. a packet compiler (16 sessions / 40k proxy tokens);
  4. reasoning scaffolds.

An LLM appears once, as a reader that can be swapped out. The paper's real contribution is measurement discipline at a one-question margin.

  • Retrieval coverage (§3): gold sessions are in the candidate pool for 468/470 answerable questions, and packets are gold-complete for 462/470.
  • Headline (§5.1): the Claude reader (CLI alias, resolved to claude-opus-5 from the transcripts) scores 479/500 and 475/500 in two passes. The pair is reported as 477±2, bracketing Chronos High's published 478.
  • 8 rows flip between the passes.
  • Wilson 95% intervals for 479, 475 and 478 overlap almost entirely (§7).
  • The judge is the noise floor (§4.2, §8):
  • re-judging the byte-identical pass-1 answers with the same official GPT-4o judge returned 478, with 3 verdict flips;
  • a second judge (GLM-5.3) agrees on 493/500 (98.6%) in each pass and scores the pair 472/472.
  • A negative control blocked a "helpful" verifier (§4.3, §6.2). A cite-or-revise verifier recovered 3 of 12 wrong rows but revised 19 of 58 correct drafts and flipped 11 to wrong. A pre-committed zero-flip rule rejected it.
  • A higher-effort agentic reader got worse (§5.1, §7). The grok-4.6 reader at max effort with an agentic transport scored 461/465, against 476/474 at high effort.
  • 28 scored rows made calls outside their prompt; one web search returned a public issue thread quoting the expected answer.
  • The regression survives removing those rows.
  • Lack of request capture is the provenance gap (§7). The headline lane disabled only the built-in tools, not MCP tools. Each call also carried about 59,000–62,000 characters of the operator's global instruction file, memory index, hook output and MCP instructions.
  • The complete outbound requests were not retained, so what the model was offered cannot be reconstructed.
  • Reader range (§5.4): on identical packets, reader choice moves the score from 93 to 479; the top four readers fall within 14 points of each other.
  • Limitations (§7):
  • every component was developed on the same 500 questions, with no held-out set;
  • one judge prompt (knowledge-update) departs in substance from the official one;
  • no human adjudication;
  • the retrieval and scaffold sources are held back.

BQAA read. For agent evals stored in BigQuery, this is a checklist of fields a run record needs before a one-point difference means anything.

  • Judge snapshot and a repeat-judge flip count.
  • Second-judge agreement.
  • Resolved reader model, not the alias.
  • The complete outbound request: tool list and injected context.
  • A negative-control replay before promoting any "improvement".

The 59–62k characters of operator context riding along on every call (§7) is exactly what a trace of the full request would expose.

NEW 3 — Agent-Warden: eBPF-based kernel-native process–file provenance tracking for LLM agents (2609.38245)

Full PDF read (9 pp.). USTC / China Greatwall; accepted at IEEE TPS 2026.

What it does. An eBPF monitor that propagates provenance markers using four rules (§IV-B, Eq. 1–2):

  • process derivation: fork and exec;
  • process → file: a successful write marks the inode;
  • file → process: a successful read marks the reader, even an unrelated process;
  • exit aggregation: a terminating child's state passes to its parent. This is a deliberate over-approximation so that short-lived proxy tasks stay linked.

Return-side hooks drop failed operations, and edges stream to user space through a ring buffer. There are two state backends: a PID-keyed hash map, and task/inode-local storage that is reclaimed with the kernel object.

  • Visibility versus an app-layer trace (§V-B, Table III). In a controlled scenario the agent spawns a script, which writes a temp file; mv renames it; then an uninstrumented host script spawns cat to read it.
  • Langfuse records the instrumented agent's events but not the process, file or rename events, and does not reconstruct the continuation through the file.
  • Agent-Warden reconstructs the full chain.
  • The authors say plainly that this reflects where instrumentation stops, not a Langfuse defect: explicitly propagated trace context would correlate.
  • Repeatability (Table IV): all six expected behaviors held 30/30 per backend on both x86-64 and ARM64.
  • Cross-temporal case (Fig. 3): a polling service read an agent-marked file about 77 s into the run, after the agent's task flow had ended, and stayed linked.
  • Overhead (Table V, n = 90 rounds): end-to-end 0.2–3.5%, extra system CPU 0.6–3.7%. x86-64 hash backend 0.2 ± 1.2%; ARM64 3.5 ± 0.4%.
  • Limitations (§VI-c):
  • no io_uring, mmap, pipes, sockets or network;
  • exit aggregation can create false-positive links, and reparenting can attribute to systemd;
  • inode labels do not survive inode-cache eviction or reboot;
  • the functional result is one predefined adversarial payload.

BQAA read. BQAA sits at the application layer (ADK plugin). This paper draws the boundary that layer cannot see past:

  • generated scripts;
  • detached children;
  • file-mediated handoffs to processes that carry no trace context.

The practical takeaways:

  • Record visibility scope. A trace row should say what its instrumentation could see, for example "tool spawned subprocess; OS-level effects not observed".
  • Plan a join key. If kernel provenance (AgentSight, Agent-Warden, Tetragon) is ever joined in, a PID/TGID plus start-time key on tool spans is the natural join. Agent-Warden's own future work (§VI-d, item 1) is exactly this cross-layer alignment.
  • Rank caveat: the evidence is thin, which is why this is NEW 3 rather than 1.

Six-week EV

  1. Gateways are becoming trace warehouses you can query. LiteLLM rc.1 puts team-scoped SQL over otel_traces / agent_traces_by_key / spend_logs, backed by ClickHouse readers.
  • Expect "bring your own SQL over agent traces, with per-team row scope" to become a baseline checklist item.
  • BQAA already has this natively. The gap to watch is per-team row policy over trace tables.
  1. "Wrongful inaction" becomes a metric. Who Verifies the Graph gives a clean, numbered case that audits of executions alone miss rejected-but-beneficial actions (97.1% lost while false executions are 0).
  • Expect decision-gate products (ToolCallJudge, DECISION spans, approval ledgers) to start logging rejections with their evidence.
  • Analytics needs a rejected-and-later-audited view.
  1. Eval records need judge-noise fields. A judge flipping 3 verdicts on byte-identical answers, plus a 98.6% cross-judge agreement, puts a floor of about 1% under any leaderboard delta. Run records should carry the judge snapshot, a re-judge flip count and the resolved reader model.
  2. Capturing the full request is becoming a reproducibility requirement. The memory paper could not reconstruct what its headline reader was sent, which included 59–62k characters of operator context and unknown MCP tools. Request-level capture, with tools offered and injected context, is the field it lacked. Agent analytics schemas can carry it.
  3. App-layer and kernel-layer provenance will need a join. Agent-Warden and AgentSight both point at it. Within weeks this stays research; the near-term action is recording subprocess PID and start time on tool spans.

  • card: A red-team of the CIVeX causal action verifier that corrupts only its committed graph.
