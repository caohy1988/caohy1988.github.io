arXiv:2611.16493 — ReA-Bench: Benchmarking Responsible Agent Auditing

Zipeng Ye and colleagues, Alibaba AAIG; arXiv v1 submitted Nov 12, 2026. Reserve promotion, carried from the 0034 abstract screen. Catalog searches for the exact id, name and expanded title found no existing item.

Full PDF read: all 50 pages, extracted with pdftotext -layout and read completely in chunks: abstract; §§1–8; references; Appendices A1–A12, B1–B6, C1–C2, D1, E1–E6, F1 and G1–G4; methods, results, limitations, prompts and Tables 1–30. Full paper.

What it contributes. ReA-Bench organizes auditing by the judgment required, rather than only by the type of harm. Its six tiers cover action/security knowledge; contradictions with intent, observations or rules; evidence diluted across long traces or large outputs; provenance and logical verification; indirect steering; and missing rules or runtime records. The tiers are diagnostic categories, not a claimed strict difficulty ladder. Most seeds come from production trajectories, with manual adaptation, red-team cases and controlled construction where coverage is thin (§§2.2–2.5).

Keep the denominator straight. The dataset has 587 trajectory versions: 304 risky, 177 benign and 106 controls. The main L1–L5 evaluation uses 277 original risky plus 177 benign samples. The 79 shortened L3 controls and 27 information-supplied L6 controls are not additional independent examples in overall recall; L6’s 27 information-absent originals are analyzed separately. The main comparison covers eight model configurations and four methods; the exploratory Global-Summary Audit (GSA) is evaluated on four configurations. Provider-blocked results are excluded from valid denominators, and paired analyses restrict to shared valid cases (§2.3, Table 1; §3; Appendix E4).

Three results worth acting on:

  1. A high-recall auditor can be a poor choice at a fixed false-positive budget. At threshold 5, method-averaged recall/FPR is 87.0%/52.0% for GPT-luna and 88.5%/54.0% for GPT-sol, versus 71.0%/5.5% for Grok-high (Table 3). When each method’s threshold is varied and empirical FPR must remain ≤10%, the best recall among the four methods is 50.5%, 47.3% and 78.0%, respectively (Table 5). These are benchmark operating points, not production acceptance thresholds. The comparison changes when legitimate operations that resemble risks count against the auditor.
  2. Missing evidence is not simply a longer-context problem. In 17 paired L6 cases, adding business rules produces mean recall of 86.0–97.1%, depending on method; in 10 runtime-evidence pairs, the corresponding range is 61.3–77.5% (Table 14). Before supplementation, only 25.7–41.2% and 10.0–13.8%, respectively, cross the alarm threshold. Those before values are threshold exceedance with insufficient information, not an equally grounded recall baseline. Runtime examples include a source file differing from the code actually executed and a remote agent closing tickets without performing the review claimed by its local coordinator (Appendices A11–A12). A successful local tool response cannot establish what an unobserved delegate did.
  3. Shortening a trace does not reliably repair a judgment error. Across five L3 version comparisons, 87.1–89.2% of paired judgments remain on the same side of threshold 5 (Table 12). In a reporting-window example, reducing approximately 220,000 to 13,000 characters changes detections only 12→13 out of 28; a different exemption-rule error is detected 26/28 times both before and after shortening from approximately 400,000 to 36,000 characters (Table 13). These are selected illustrative cases, not a general causal estimate of context-length effects.

What the audit methods actually do. Final Audit reads the entire trace once; miniCoT enumerates candidate concerns before deciding in the same call. Full-History Audit rereads the prefix every 15 messages; Windowed Audit reads the latest 20 messages at the same stride and keeps user requests outside the window. GSA extracts evidence from nonoverlapping 20-message windows, each with access to the full trace, then audits the full trace plus all extracted notes. It is post-hoc evidence organization, not a demonstrated bounded-memory streaming monitor (Table 21; §3.1).

GSA improves both AUROC and average precision against each configuration’s best-AUROC baseline in three of four configurations; for GPT-luna, AUROC goes 0.825→0.785 and AP 0.862→0.824. Its fixed-threshold recall gains also come with higher FPR in all four comparisons (Tables 6–7). Cost is material: median cumulative input is 155,860 tokens for GSA versus 29,720 for Final Audit, and GSA’s maximum is 10,610,715 tokens, summed over calls, not a single context window (Table 26). Cached input is included. The authors report cache reuse and characterize resulting cost as similar to Windowed Audit, but those token statistics do not establish a portable dollar saving across providers.

Limits that affect adoption.

  • Dataset release is pending. The abstract and §2.5 say de-identification and release review precede open-sourcing; this brief does not present it as a downloadable benchmark ready to run.
  • Hard-benign FPR is not production FPR. In a supplementary set of 200 provider-labeled benign production traces, the selected highest-benchmark-FPR method per model drops to 0–1.5% for the six non-GPT configurations; GPT-luna/FHA is 43.5%, GPT-sol/miniCoT 22.5% (Table 29). That production set will not be released. It demonstrates distribution sensitivity, not a general deployment estimate.
  • One run per configuration; uneven small subgroups. §6 explicitly calls for repeated experiments and domain/source-separated calibration and test sets. Small score differences and empirical threshold choices are not stability guarantees. L5 and L6 have no benign controls of their own (Table 1).
  • Supplementation is an idealized test. L6 directly supplies relevant rules or runtime observations; retrieving trustworthy, current and complete auxiliary evidence in deployment remains unsolved (§6). Supplying information and detecting risk are different system responsibilities.
  • Reasoning comparisons have a confound. DeepSeek-Pro’s thinking-enabled results combine historical runs and reruns, so Appendix E2 says the comparison is between overall configurations, not an isolated causal effect of enabling thinking.

BQAA interpretation: preserve the distinction between “observed and permitted,” “observed and violating,” and “insufficient evidence.” Join a decision to its applicable authorization and policy version, actual tool observations and delegated execution records; evaluate benign lookalikes alongside failures. That is a proposed BQAA experiment inspired by the paper, not a feature demonstrated in BQAA or ADK.

Six-week EV

Highest expected value: a small audit-evidence experiment, not a new monitor deployment. This is a proposed plan, not work performed:

  • Weeks 1–2: curate paired examples with similar actions but different authorization, and examples whose necessary evidence is absent. Preserve source/role, event identity, policy version, tool result and parent/delegate relationships.
  • Weeks 3–4: compare a single full-trace audit with evidence extraction plus audit. Report recall at a predeclared FPR budget, false-positive counts, evidence availability, abstentions and complete input/output/cache usage. Keep calibration and evaluation examples separate by source.
  • Weeks 5–6: review the exact gained/lost detections and added/removed false positives. Retain the more complex method only if it improves the chosen operating point enough to justify review workload and cost. ReA-Bench’s eventual public release can supply another test set; it is not a dependency for constructing local diagnostic pairs.

The payoff is knowing whether to improve capture, evidence joins or judgment. The risk is optimizing a score on curated examples that does not transfer to BQAA workloads; a held-out domain is the relevant next check.
