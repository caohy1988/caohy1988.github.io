Replay fidelity (2611.11216) shows that whether a stored agent execution "reproduces" depends on which projection you check — printed outputs, database deltas, or verdict — and that accepted replays can still diverge on the next tool call through omitted RNG and credential state. Mutation testing for multi-agent workflows (2611.09895) measures whether a test suite would catch coordination faults at all: task-success-style suites detect only about half of permission-relaxation faults, because nothing in them ever probes a forbidden agent–tool pair. Done Is Not Correct (2611.10063) prices the completion claim itself under an exact oracle: unverified agents were silently wrong on 14.1% of claimed completions, and a measure-then-claim harness contract — not the feedback alone — cut that to 3.2%. The common BQAA requirement: traces and claims need typed evidence attached (replay contract, intervention/probe identity, measurement-before-claim), or downstream eval, debugging, and audit numbers are uninterpretable.

NEW 1 — Replay Fidelity in LLM Agent Evaluation: Randomness, Hidden State, and Test Oracles in AppWorld — https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.11216

Full PDF read (pdftotext, all 11 pages: Sections 1–11, Tables 1–5, Figures 1–3, threats to validity, and references).

Liu and Lu audit every one of the 71 unsuccessful executions in a fixed historical AppWorld corpus with 284 isolated fixed-code replays (Section 2, Table 1), crossing two reconstruction controls — N (reconstructed declared settings, seed 123) vs C (additionally reseeding the RNG before each interaction) — against three validation targets: printed-output equality (O), pre-oracle database-delta equality (D_pre), and native verdict/test-count agreement (V). The headline is the interaction, not a single replay rate (Section 3, Figure 1, Table 2): strict admission (O ∧ D_pre ∧ V) is 42/71 under N vs 68/71 under C, a 26-case gap, but it contracts to 4 cases under D_pre ∧ V and to 0 under V alone — most of the strict gap is token-expiry text variation that never reaches the endpoint state. The four residual discrepancies are real data, not serialization: materialized phone-table row fingerprints differ between N and C in both repetitions while outputs and verdicts match (Section 4, Table 3).

The mechanism studies are the part BQAA should internalize. On one endpoint pair accepted by all three checks, an identical literal API call returns the same HTTP 200 and printed response yet writes different rows, because the two reconstructions carry different Python RNG stream positions; aligning future RNG removes the difference in both paired repetitions (Section 5, Figure 2). A second program isolates a credential-value effect — under C the endpoint's current token equals the initially visible shared token, under N it does not — flipping a shared-token read/write between 200 and 401 with a playlist-row difference, with forced-binding controls and one infrastructure-censored pair retained as missing (Section 6, Table 4). Separately, a naïve post-oracle export check would wrongly reject 10 C cases because the in-process oracle rebinds ORM classes; exporting from retained pre-oracle trackers restores the comparison on all 10 without touching cached rows (Section 7, Figure 3). A 12-case BFCL pilot finds no discrepancy under its public-state checks — a boundary, not a prevalence estimate (Section 8).

Why it matters for BQAA: any trace store that later supports replay, counterfactual debugging, or failure attribution inherits this contract problem. "Replayable" is meaningless without naming the reference object (archived agent-end deltas here, not a gold terminal state), the observation stage (pre- vs post-oracle), and the randomness coupling; verdict agreement can coexist with row-level divergence that changes the very next action. The authors are disciplined about scope (Section 9.3): one old AppWorld version, one harness/model lineage, 32 related families, mechanism cases selected for explanatory value, and no estimate of debugger-accuracy or ranking effects. Card it as a replay-contract audit method (admission ladder + materialized-state checks + retained unknowns), not as a transferable replay rate.

NEW 2 — Mutation Testing for Multi-Agent Workflows — https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.09895

Full PDF read (pdftotext, all 11 pages: Sections I–VI, Tables I–VI, Figure 3, threats to validity). In the Nov 10 batch as a new cs.SE listing; the paper itself is dated 25 Jun 2026.

Kahani and Bagherzadeh adapt mutation analysis from program source to the coordination structure of a multi-agent workflow. Fourteen first-order operators in four families perturb a normalized workflow manifest — delegation (remove/redirect/invert/add), allowed-tool (remove/redirect/fault-inject/signature-rename), restriction (relax/tighten/remove), and agent/instruction (remove/swap prompts/drop prompt) (Section III-B, Table I) — and three execution-decided kill criteria score whether a suite catches the change: K-Struct (observed tools/restricted events/delegations differ), K-Outcome (verdict or ordered step sequence differs), and K-Safety (a forbidden agent–tool call actually fires) (Section III-D). On ten OpenAI Agents SDK-derived workflows (49 reachable agents, 47 tools, 403 obligations; Table II), 1,084 capped mutants and 29,601 agent executions (k=3, majority vote) compare three practice-proxy suites against a full-obligation structural reference suite (T-Struct, 1.000 by construction and used only as the gap denominator).

The gaps are the result (Tables IV–VI, Figure 3). Under K-Struct the best baseline (developer smoke slice) reaches 0.717, the task-success-style suite 0.560, and a random 10% suite 0.488. Under K-Safety the baselines fall to 0.559 / 0.503 / 0.401: catching a silently relaxed restriction requires a test that drives the agent at the forbidden pair, which positive-polarity suites do only by accident. Restriction-removal is the least detected operator for every baseline (0.19–0.28 vs 1.000), because it broadens capability without breaking any final answer; K-Outcome adds almost nothing over K-Struct in magnitude (0.717→0.725) but is what makes obligation-preserving operators (tool-fault, prompt-swap, prompt-drop) scorable at all — a manifest-only oracle cannot witness them. Difficulty rankings are stable across K-Struct and K-Outcome, pointing at fault type rather than oracle choice.

Why it matters for BQAA: this is the missing adequacy metric for agent eval suites. Passing a task benchmark says nothing about whether the suite would detect a removed handoff, a misrouted tool edge, or a dropped permission guard — exactly the regressions a workflow edit introduces. For an analytics store, the actionable import is the obligation inventory (reach / use-tool / delegate / restrict-probe per workflow) as first-class trace metadata: coverage and kill rates per obligation type are computable from stored executions only if delegations, tool calls, and restriction events are recorded as typed structure. Limits are stated (Section IV-I): one framework and one agent model (gpt-4.1-mini), mutants executed only on structurally incident obligations, per-operator caps, and flaky-kill variance retained rather than suppressed. Card the operator catalog + kill-criteria design and the baseline gaps; do not transfer the absolute scores across frameworks.

NEW 3 — Done Is Not Correct: Measuring Silent Failures and Self-Verification Calibration When LLM Agents Take CAD Actions — https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.10063

Full PDF read (pdftotext, all 18 pages: Sections 1–8, Tables 1–2, Figures 1–3, error modes, limitations, and Appendices A–C including verbatim prompts and the reproducibility checklist). Dated 6 Nov 2026; first seen in this batch above the prior ceiling.

Rodrigues et al. build the cleanest version yet of a claim-conditioned reliability metric: 13 natural-language CAD specification tasks whose every requirement maps to machine-checkable properties of the produced solid (122 checks, published tolerance bands), graded by an exact kernel oracle with no judge model in the loop (Sections 3–4). The silent-failure rate is the conjunction of valid renderable geometry, an explicit agent completion claim, and at least one failed semantic check; completion-claim calibration (ECE, AUROC, Brier) is reported alongside claim rate and claim precision. Across four models from four vendors (n=156 per architecture, task-cluster intervals, paired McNemar tests), single-shot agents claimed completion on 153/156 attempts and were silently wrong on 22 (14.1%, cluster CI [5.8, 24.4]; claim precision 64.1%) (F1, Table 1).

The intervention result is about harness contract, not iteration. A tool-use loop that refuses a completion claim until the agent has seen kernel measurements of its final code cuts silent failures to 5/156 (3.2%, p<10⁻⁴) and raises claim precision to 96.0%; the single-variable ablation — same feedback, legacy claim contract — produces 14 silent failures and 67.6% precision, attributing the effect to the contract rather than the feedback (F1). A planner–coder–checker pipeline matches precision (95.5%) only by claiming far less often (57.1% vs 80.8% coverage) with no success gain (F2). Stated confidence is saturated and miscalibrated single-shot (ECE 0.324) and becomes informative under verification for three of four models; the fourth reports 1.0 regardless of outcome, separating claim discipline from stated uncertainty (F3). The benchmark audits itself both ways: hardening the oracle flipped 18 of 519 regraded verdicts pass→fail and none in reverse, while a blind expert audit overturned 3 of the 18 silent failures outside the one spec-ambiguous task — rates bounded on both sides (F6) — and the contrast survives scaling every tolerance band from ×0.25 to ×4 (F7, Table 2). Negative results are reported plainly: context-divergence between roles predicts breakdown no better than episode length and misses valid-but-wrong parts (F4), and a pre-specified capability prediction failed (Section 8).

Why it matters for BQAA: CAD is the instrument, not the claim. Wherever an exact or near-exact checker exists (SQL result validation, schema/constraint checks, test execution), the transferable design is (a) score the agent's own completion claim against the checker — claim precision and silent-failure rate as stored trace fields, not just task success — and (b) enforce measurement-before-claim in the harness, which the ablation shows is the active ingredient. Limits: 13 tasks with failures concentrated in two, one ambiguously specified (contrast survives excluding it), four pinned models with no OpenAI/Google model in the primary population, one prompt per condition, and a single co-author expert rater (Section 8). Card the metric + contract finding, not the absolute CAD rates.

Six-week EV

  • Replay contracts for stored traces (2611.11216): High EV, cheap to start. Add to any BQAA replay/debugging surface an explicit admission ladder — which projections were compared (observations, state deltas, verdict), at which stage (pre- vs post-evaluator), under what randomness coupling — and keep immutable pre-evaluator snapshots so the checker cannot contaminate the comparison. The paper's own guidance (Section 9.4) is directly adoptable; the open work is which projections suffice for a declared continuation family.
  • Eval-suite adequacy via workflow mutation (2611.09895): High EV for ADK/BQAA test design. The immediate, low-cost slice is negative-polarity probes: one test per restricted agent–tool pair, since restriction faults are precisely what task-success suites miss (0.19–0.28 detection). Medium-term, an obligation inventory per workflow (reach/use/delegate/restrict) turns stored traces into coverage and kill-rate reporting.
  • Claim-conditioned reliability + measure-then-claim (2611.10063): High EV wherever BQAA-adjacent agents self-report completion. Pilot: log completion claim + stated confidence + checker verdict as trace fields for one workflow with an exact checker, report silent-failure rate and claim precision weekly, and gate the claim on having seen the checker's measurement. The ablation says the gate, not extra feedback turns, is what pays.
