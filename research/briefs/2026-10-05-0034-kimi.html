NEW — 2610.02994 — Sentry: failure knowledge is conditional; exposing the playbook only on a matching, verified failure beats always-on memory

Full PDF read (methods §3, results §5 with Tables 1–5 and Fig 3, limitations §6). Stanford (Ji, Lu, Zhang, Olukotun). v1 submitted Fri 2 Oct 2026; appears in the Monday Oct 5 listing as a cs.LG cross-list.

The finding first: failure lessons kept in context misfire when their failure is absent. Removing failure-mode lessons from ACE's evolving playbook improves held-out performance (WebShop reward 0.335→0.391; Mind2Web Replay completion 0.562→0.643, §2), and exposing the same frozen playbook persistently lowers WebShop reward 0.364→0.296 and M2W completion 0.651→0.599 (Table 4). Intervening without a need check drops reward below the unassisted agent (0.136 vs. 0.170, §2).

The system: Sentry, a failure-management layer beside the agent. It routes invalid actions to hard repair, diagnoses progress/reasoning-grounding failures over the last W=5 cycles, retrieves at most k=5 lessons matching the failure type + labels, and writes a new lesson only after verifying — without access to task rewards — that the agent recovered over the next H=10 cycles. The full playbook never enters the agent's context.

Results (Qwen3.5-9B, five runs, online-from-scratch / held-out protocols §4.3): beats the strongest runtime-intervention baseline on all four benchmarks — WebShop +77.3%, AppWorld +39.8%, SWE-bench Lite +25.0%, Mind2Web Replay +7.1% (Table 1; 37% average) — and beats ACE by +47.8% / +29.7% (Table 2; the abstract's "39%" is the two-benchmark average). ACE+Sentry is best overall, so task-level and failure-level memory compose. Paired forks from 200 intervention points: local recovery 33.5%→60.5%, final score better in 74 / tie 101 / worse 25 (§5.2). Human agreement: 86.0% on interventions, 81.8% on recovery judgments (κ=0.82–0.89, §5.2). Cost: 1.54× base-agent tokens (Table 5a). Ablations (Table 3): removing the intervention-need check is the biggest hit (−70.9% WebShop / −38.5% AppWorld); removing recovery verification −26.7%/−13.1%.

Limitations (§6): only Qwen3.5-9B and GPT-OSS-120B; the same model plays task agent, detector and verifier (decoupling unstudied); the playbook can accumulate redundant/over-specific entries.

Why it ranks. First controlled demonstration that when failure knowledge enters the context matters as much as its content — a direct design constraint on every agent-memory feature that defaults to "always include the lessons."

NEW — 2610.02267 — Fast Models, Slow Evidence: a paired, self-audited evaluation of System-1 decision models — JEV wins 9 of 11 harness decision points; the authors' own audit moves the headline saving from 23.9% to 4.3%

Full PDF read (protocol §3, benchmark §4/Table 1, results §5–6 with Figs 1–5 and Tables 3–8, self-audit §7/Table 2, threats §8, recommendations/limitations §9). Jiawei Li. v1 1 Oct 2026. Artifacts: github.com/David-DL-Space/sys1-eval.

The eval: 11 harness decision points (model routing, tool selection, tool-call guard, RAG relevance, injection guard, large label sets, groundedness, pre-execution safety, PII egress, plus controls) built from 18 public sources — 7,283 base cases + 6,640 robustness variants, byte-identical inputs to both systems, exact McNemar paired tests, Wilson intervals, cross-hardware and cross-day reproducibility checks (JEV changed 5/1,080 decisions across days; LAYA 1,180/1,180 across GPU→CPU).

Findings (Fig 1, §5): JEV is significantly more accurate on 9 of 11 decision points (+10.8 to +46.0 pp); overall 76.7% vs 63.1%. The two ties are the instructive ones: zero-shot model routing is at chance for both (JEV answers "cheap" for 99.5% of requests; LAYA AUROC 0.48), and RAG relevance is a well-powered tie (+0.5 pp, CI [−2.0, 3.0]) where the systems err in opposite directions (JEV recall 58.3% vs 45.4%; LAYA rejects more hard negatives). LAYA is order-sensitive — reversing option order flips 30.3% of its decisions (53% with 60–77 labels) vs 1.8% for JEV (Fig 3a) — and collapses on many similar candidates: 31.3% at K=50 nearest-neighbour tools vs 98.9% for JEV on unique-correct items (Fig 2, Table 7). On pre-execution safety LAYA lets through 81.5% of harmful requests (JEV 25.5%); its PII AUROC of 0.41 ranks classes the wrong way round (Table 3).

The self-audit is the card-worthy half (§7, Table 2): re-deriving every number from raw outputs found that the reported 23.9% safety-review saving was actually 4.3% once the pre-screen's own tokens are counted (Table 8: τ=0.1 costs 95.7% of LLM-only; τ=0.05 costs 4.7% more); that "end-to-end correctness 45–58%" was gate classification accuracy mislabelled (97.9% of answerable queries retained); that in-sample 5%-FNR thresholds blow out to p95 6–17% held-out (Fig 4b); and that a "channel effect" on injection false positives vanishes with channel-native content (0%→0% FPR for JEV on native documents, Fig 6). Cost/latency: JEV ≈ $32 per million decisions (759 tokens/decision at $0.042/M), p50 83–325 ms depending on client site; LAYA 31 ms on GPU, 200 ms on M4 CPU (§5).

Limitations (§9): two systems at single versions, zero-shot, English-dominant public data with synthetic mixes; ±5 pp wording band on any single accuracy number; JEV's training data is not public, so near-perfect scores on popular sets may partly reflect exposure; LAYA's fine-tuning path untested (its typed-decisions checkpoint crashed).

Why it ranks. The first paired, reproducibility-checked evaluation of the System-1/typed-decision layer the fleet itself runs (Jev) — and a transferable checklist: the errors that changed deployment recommendations sat in the accounting around the model, not its scored predictions.

NEW — 2610.02363 — ArrivalBench: snapshot grading certifies 86–100% of agent-written data pipelines; replay under late/duplicate/out-of-order delivery finds 7.0–79.2% of the certified ones silently wrong

Full PDF read (method §3, results §4 with Tables 1–6 and Figs 2–3, discussion/limitations §5, full limitations Appendix B). Pranay Kothari (Oxford); NeurIPS 2026 "Who Verifies the Agents?" workshop. v1 1 Oct 2026.

The design: ask an agent to write an incremental data pipeline, then grade the artifact after the agent has stopped by re-executing it under adversarial-but-replayable delivery schedules (late arrival, out-of-order CDC, duplicate delivery, crash-and-retry, backfill, additive schema evolution — each grounded in a verbatim primary-documentation quote). The oracle is a batch recomputation over the complete logical log, so a wrong table and a crash are distinct verdicts (FAIL vs ERROR) — a crash is visible to monitoring a team already runs; a wrong table is not. 40 tasks, 42 negative controls, k=5 samples, 11 models / 8 organisations, 18 arms, 28,041 gradeable verdicts that re-grade byte-identically with zero mismatches (§4.1).

Results (Table 1, Fig 2): the authors' reimplementation of single-execution snapshot grading certifies 86–100% of pipelines across eleven models; replay finds 7.0–79.2% of certified pipelines silently wrong — an elevenfold separation the incumbent metric cannot see (GPT-5.6 Sol 7.0%, Opus 5 16.7%, Kimi K3 35.2%, Sonnet 5 48.2%, Haiku 4.5 52.2%, Codestral 79.2%). The gap is not produced by the repair loop: within the same model and task (163 strata), cells repaired against the snapshot test fail replay no more than first-time passes (+0.2 pp [−6.6, +6.6], §4.2). Signature (§4.3, Fig 3): idempotency hazards fail more often than ordering hazards in all eleven unhinted arms on group means (+4.0 to +39.4 pp; 15/18 arms positive with 3 saturated ties). The FAIL/ERROR separation changes how interventions read: a hazard-warning prompt cuts one model's silent failure 48.2%→10.5% while raising crashes 9.0%→37.0% — all-in failure moves only 51.0%→44.0%, i.e., the prompt converted silent failure into loud failure rather than removing it (§4.5). All eleven unhinted arms were independently re-run; rates moved at most 5.9 points.

Limitations (§5, App B): 40 single-author tasks and golds sharing one anti-join staging idiom; one engine/dialect (DuckDB SQL); open-weights arms via one aggregator with no intervention arms; evaluation code, tasks and pre-registration not public with this version; three pilot claims failed to replicate at 40 tasks and are withdrawn.

Why it ranks. It turns "the pipeline passed the benchmark" into a verdict about day-two behavior, and it names the gap every agent-coded-artifact benchmark currently ignores: pass-once grading certifies pipelines that corrupt the warehouse on redelivery.

NEW — 2610.02932 — When to Compile a Computer-Use Agent? PACE measures payback (2–16 uses) and makes budgeted compile decisions, saving 17–25% of tokens

Full PDF read (method §3, measurement §4/Tables 1–3, ablations Table 4, cost-assumption sensitivity §5, limitations §7). City University of Hong Kong (Ming, Xu, Wu, Jia). v1 2 Oct 2026. Code: github.com/myl7/computer-use.

The problem: compiling a repeated GUI procedure into a program saves model tokens per run, but compilation cost is uncertain (attempts need repair and can still fail) and future reuse is unknown (tasks stop arriving; GUI drift kills the program). PACE records successful and failed compilation costs, compares agent vs program execution on matched inputs to estimate per-use savings and payback counts, and an online rule checks execution/compilation charges against a cumulative budget from observed arrivals before allowing either — total cost after each arrival stays ≤ (1+ε) × the all-agent cost under stated action-cost assumptions (§4.3).

Numbers: estimated payback counts excluding source runs are 2–16 uses across programs (Table 1; 1.389–15.488). In simulations on recorded arrival sequences, PACE reduces token cost 17.3% vs ReAct, 24.9% vs an AutoRPA adaptation, 17.3% vs a ToolPro adaptation (ε=0.25; abstract, Table 3); over two 300-condition grids ReAct costs 1.107–1.130× PACE (Table 2). Ablations (Table 4, §5): removing the budget barely moves mean cost on recorded logs (0.0013%) but raises the worst grid ratio to ReAct from 1.2468 to 1.7563 (+40.9%) — the budget is tail insurance, not mean savings. Assigning high compilation costs to frequent-but-flaky families pushes PACE to 1.5% above ReAct — task frequency alone is not enough to decide (§5).

Limitations (§7): seven task families, three model profiles, simulations use given family labels and fixed cost profiles; the live GUI study tests one fixed program, not the full online algorithm; token cost only — latency, device use and program maintenance excluded.

Why it ranks. The first payback-accounted, budget-constrained answer to "when is procedure compilation worth it" — the same question every agent-cost platform asks about caching, distillation and routing.

Six-week EV

  1. Agent memory gets a conditional-exposure rule. Sentry's paired findings — persistent playbook exposure hurts (0.364→0.296), failure-triggered verified-only retrieval helps (+37% avg) — give memory products a concrete design constraint: retrieval timing (on diagnosed failure) and admission gating (verified recovery) beat retrieval quality alone. Expect "when to surface" to become a first-class knob in agent-memory systems within six weeks, and watch for harnesses adding failure-detection spans to drive it.
  2. Eval methodology is becoming the product surface. Two of this window's four papers flip their own headlines by fixing accounting (23.9%→4.3% savings; 86–100% certified vs 7–79% silently wrong). The durable lesson for analytics: grade the artifact under re-execution hazards, count the decision layer's own tokens in any savings claim, and pick thresholds out of sample. Products that certify agent outputs from single snapshot runs will increasingly read as marketing, not measurement.
  3. Cost decisions are formalizing into budgeted online rules. PACE's payback counts (2–16 uses) and (1+ε) budget, the sys1 paper's call-rate/held-out-miss-rate gate accounting, and last slot's cache-write token fields (openai-node 7.28.0) are the same move at three layers: every "savings" mechanism (compile, gate, cache) must publish its own cost and its payback horizon. Expect agent-cost telemetry to standardize per-decision cost lines so these audits can run on production data.
