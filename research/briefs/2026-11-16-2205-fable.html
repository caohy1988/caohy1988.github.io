Industry

Anthropic: "Building strong safety evaluations"

Ruth Appel, Evan Frondorf, Jeremy Hadfield, Rebecca Hiscott, Kamya Jagadish, Shayne Longpre, Madeleine Tucker and Brett Wittmershaus, 2026-11-13. Source: https://www.anthropic.com/research/building-strong-safety-evaluations

What it is. Recommendations for safety evals, which ask whether a model "did the right thing", with examples from Anthropic's system cards.

Recommendations.

  1. Team. Involve methods experts (sampling, grading, analysis) and domain experts (what counts as harm) from the start.
  2. Running safely. Safeguards are often disabled, so sandbox without internet access and monitor in real time. After its own incidents, the lab paused many internet-enabled evals.
  3. Construct. For self-harm conversations refusal is often the wrong construct: a model refusing every such prompt gets a perfect refusal-rate score but is not necessarily safer. The lab's multi-turn evals report an "appropriate response rate" against expert rubrics, where one bad turn fails the conversation. Pair harmful with benign prompts to catch over-refusal.
  4. Grader validation. LLM graders can break silently. Use yes/no criteria, hide scenario metadata from the grader, flag and exclude items it breaks on, and re-grade a sample with a different model. Have humans label a sample blind: grader-expert agreement should roughly match expert-expert agreement.
  5. Uncertainty. Preregister metrics, comparisons, slices and thresholds. The lab's floors: 100 items for a multi-turn eval, 1,000 for single-turn. Report confidence intervals and per-category results, including unchanged categories.
  6. Disclosure. Report model version, sampling settings, prompts, grader version and dates; never compare scores across graders. Give the developer time to fix a severe flaw before publishing.

If you only do three things. Write down the construct before collecting data, validate the grader against human judgment before trusting its numbers, and report uncertainty.

Examples. Aggregate single-turn harmless response rates are 94% to 98% for every recent model on the API: saturated, useful mainly as regression checks. On a multi-turn romance-scams eval, Claude Opus 5 and Opus 5.5 differed (98% versus 94%) without statistical significance. For Claude Fable 5.1, multi-turn results on the raw API and claude.ai differed by up to 34 percentage points.

Why it matters for BQAA/ADK. Writer's reading: the grader and reporting advice turns into questions any eval record must answer. Which system prompt, tools, surface and grader version produced a score? Does the judge agree with blind human labels? Does each rate carry an interval and a per-category breakdown?
