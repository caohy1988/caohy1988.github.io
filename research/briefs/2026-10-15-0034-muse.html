Divergent Paths: Learning an Outcome-Predictive Representation of LLM Agent Trajectories

  • Full PDF read. v1 [cs.LG] 14 Oct 2026. Gill, Gogia & Guzmán (Handshake AI / U. Utah). Code: github.com/alexgill321/precog-rm (named in paper; not audited by me).
  • The question: agent trajectories run 10–100+ turns and 100K–500K+ tokens, and most downstream machinery (credit assignment, failure diagnosis, data filtering) reads the raw token sequence. The paper reframes a trajectory as a path through a shared embedding space of actions and observations (the AOS) and asks whether outcomes are separable in that space early enough to act on.
  • Method: per-task discriminators (nearest-centroid / RBF on pooled representations; fastText on clustered state-ID sequences) fit per task under stratified 3-fold CV (~33 training rollouts), then Precog-RM — a transformer meta-learner that performs outcome prediction few-shot from a small support set of labeled rollouts with no per-task fitting. Three agent benchmarks × three policy models; finally Precog-RM is deployed as an early-stopping controller for GRPO on Tau2-Bench, leaving optimizer and advantage estimator untouched.
  • Results: outcomes are separable in every condition — best per-task discriminator 0.77–0.95 AUROC on held-out rollouts (Table 1), with ceilings shifting ~0.12 AUROC across benchmarks and ~0.05 across models (AgentClinic the consistent low performer). Precog-RM's margin is largest at k=1 (+0.06–0.12 AUROC) and at k=2 it matches or exceeds the strongest baselines at k=8 — a four-fold reduction in labeled rollouts (Figure 3); cross-model generalization to unseen policies/benchmarks is reported in Table 2 (OOD cells shaded). As a GRPO controller it cuts training FLOPs by up to 47.3% while keeping pass@1 within one standard deviation of baseline.
  • Limits (from the paper's own reporting): separability is not uniform (benchmark-dependent ceilings; AgentClinic lags); per-task fitting transfers nothing and is bounded by per-task rollout counts — the meta-learner exists precisely because of that bound; the GRPO result is a single-benchmark (Tau2-Bench) deployment. I found no standalone Limitations section; these bounds are stated in the results/discussion text I read.
  • Why it matters for BQAA: this prices the warehouse. If a handful of labeled rollouts per task suffices to predict outcomes of unlabeled ones at 0.9+ AUROC, then stored trajectories need (a) outcome labels joinable at rollout granularity and (b) action/observation sequences preserved in an embeddable form — not just final scores. It also gives early-stopping a measurable contract: kill doomed rollouts mid-flight and bank ~47% of training FLOPs, provided the predictor's per-task support set is versioned like any other eval artifact.

Doc2Harness: Benchmarking LLMs on Long-Horizon Harness Engineering of Coding Agents

  • Full PDF read. v1 [cs.SE] 14 Oct 2026. Ge et al. (ICT/CAS, Tsinghua, PKU, Southeast U.).
  • The question: coding-agent benchmarks fix the harness and vary the model. Doc2Harness inverts it: can a model build the harness — tools, commands, permission modes, persistent memory, exit codes — from documentation alone?
  • Method: each of 19 build models runs inside a fixed builder harness (OpenCode) and receives a development document (rewritten/merged/disambiguated from twelve frontier harnesses' user docs, to reduce parametric-knowledge shortcuts) plus a contract skeleton. Three independently built candidate harnesses per model are scored against 328 test cases (positive controls excluded from every denominator; vacuous-pass channels ruled out by three control classes); build models are ranked by mean pass rate, with each stage holding one component fixed to reduce confounding.
  • Results: pass rates span 27.44% (weakest) to 53.05% (GPT-6 Astra) — a ~26-point gap; even the strongest model fails nearly half the suite (Table 2/Figure 1). Rankings are unstable across categories (up to 16 positions). Permissions is the most discriminating category: 43.7% average pass rate, 50.8-point strong–weak gap, 51 of 61 cases discriminating; file operations averages similar (39.6%) with only a 20.9-point gap; interactive-terminal averages 79.9% and nears saturation; 56 cases are failed by every candidate (Table 3).
  • Limits (stated by the authors): the protocol measures a single build (up to three submissions), not an iterative build–evaluate–repair loop; contract checks test form rather than behavior, so an early submitter's pass rate is a lower bound; a single defect can fail several test cases at once (single-variable expansions share features).
  • Why it matters for BQAA: harness variance is the confounder behind "same model, different score" (cf. the catalog's same-model-harness card). Doc2Harness quantifies the harness as an artifact with its own capability gradient — and its category split says permission/role machinery is where harnesses actually diverge. For trace analytics, harness identity+version belongs in the primary key of any cross-agent comparison; this paper is the evidence that omitting it can swing results by double digits.

One Monitor Does Not Fit All: Formalism-Agnostic Runtime Verification for Agentic AI

  • Full PDF read. v1 [cs.AI] 14 Oct 2026. Ferrando (U. Modena and Reggio Emilia).
  • The question: runtime monitors for agents need different observations (temporal order vs. payload content) and different verdict vocabularies, yet must combine into one allow/block decision before an operation takes effect — across LangGraph, MCP and A2A boundaries.
  • Method: AgenticRV separates execution boundaries from monitoring formalism: adapters emit canonical events, each monitor receives its own projection/abstraction, and a decision policy arbitrates normalised verdicts into a reaction (allow / log / block / replace) that the responsible adapter supports (Table 1: decision vs. observation boundaries). The paper formalises when observations suffice, when monitor replacement preserves decisions, and when blocking prevents unsafe effects — explicitly distinguishing rejected attempts from completed effects so a blocked request need not leave every monitor in permanent violation.
  • Results: a controlled LangGraph experiment pairs an external Reelay temporal monitor with a programmatic report monitor; ablations over seven paths / 34 tool decisions per configuration (6 unsafe, 28 safe; labels fixed by controlled inputs and checked against an effect ledger, not by monitor verdicts) show both monitors are needed for the selected requirements (Table 2); cross-boundary validation varies temporal observation/scope partition over 24 reporting decisions with the ledger confirming release/suppression and no monitor/delivery failures (Table 3). Overhead microbenchmarks: instrumenting a one-node graph adds ~696µs over its ~223µs native run, a first monitor a further ~414µs (complete invocation 1.333ms); 0→8 immediate monitors at a guarded tool boundary adds ~869µs (Table 4).
  • Limits (stated in the paper): coverage requires using the wrapper/handler (a raw provider HTTP client needs separate instrumentation; the LangGraph proxy does not guard every reducer/state merge); results hold "subject to the stated observation and deployment assumptions"; the controlled counts concern constructed requirements/inputs, and the LLM-driven incident-response example is illustrative rather than a field study.
  • Why it matters for BQAA: monitors are producers of verdict events, and this paper gives them a storage-worthy shape: canonical events per boundary, per-monitor projections, normalised verdicts, and an effect ledger separating attempts from completions. That attempt/effect distinction is exactly what approval/audit analytics keep missing (cf. verdict-staleness in the catalog): a blocked tool call and an executed one must never aggregate as the same event class.

Retention (prune), provenance (attempt/effect), and continuity (resume) policies will be asked for together.
