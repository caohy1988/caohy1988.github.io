1. arXiv:2611.13140 — Agreeing on the Answer but Not the Reason: Measurement Limits of LLM Reasoning Failure Taxonomies

Reserve promotion (from the slot's reserve queue, abstract-level; full-PDF-read before ranking, as required). Submitted Mon 9 Nov 2026; cs.AI (primary), cs.CL. Single author, Madhu Sudhan Rao Beesetty (PayPal, San Jose). Full PDF read — all 12 pages: Abstract, §I Introduction, §II Related Work, §III Method (A Data, B Pipeline, C Taxonomy and Its Tie-Break, D Model Panel and Its Confounds), §IV Results (A The Construct as Measured, B The Plurality Is Set by the Tie-Break, C Raters Agree on the Answer Not the Reason, D Benchmark Translation Defects), §V Discussion, §VI Limitations, §VII Conclusion, Acknowledgment and AI Disclosure, References [1]–[26], Appendix A Inspector Instrument, Appendix B Generation Configuration, Appendix C Data Availability. Tables I–IV all read.

Why it ranks. The authors set out to publish a familiar result — a named reasoning failure mode ("Compositional Collapse": the model notices a required sub-part, then drops or misapplies it) dominating across twelve models and eleven languages on mGSM-Symbolic — and then found the result did not survive verification. The paper reports the verification failure instead. Three findings, each with a number I checked against the tables:

  1. The reported plurality is set by the classifier's test order (§IV-B, Table I). Evidence fields are populated independently, so 50.1% of failures (775 of 1,546) carry evidence for more than one category, and the overlap is almost entirely one pair (761 of 775 are Collapse + Drift). The classifier assigns one label per item via ordered tests returning on first match. Re-running with Drift tested first, changing nothing else, moves the nominal plurality in eleven of twelve models (Table I). Sweeping all orderings — 4! = 24 permutations collapse to 3! = 6 distinct classifiers because the residual category cannot move — Collapse's share of failures spans 26.4% to 76.4% and Drift's spans 14.9% to 64.7%, roughly fifty-point swings; Collapse wins the plurality under exactly three of the six. The authors' own summary: "Collapse is the dominant failure mode" is close to a restatement of "we tested Collapse first." They withdraw the ordered claim and report the ordering-invariant quantity instead (share of failures carrying Collapse evidence at all: 50.0%–90.2%).
  2. Raters agree on the answer, not on the reason (§IV-C, Tables II and III). On the identical 179-item sample, native speakers of all eleven languages agree with the fixed inspector at κ = 0.932 on answer correctness but κ = 0.093 on the category; the two model judges land at κ = 0.348 (DeepSeek-V4-Pro) and κ = 0.049 (Qwen2.5-32b) on correctness-vs-category splits of the same shape (answer κ 0.917 / 0.854). The low κ is not a marginals artifact: raw agreement is 50.7% against 45.7% expected by chance, and PABAK = 0.014 and Gwet's AC1 = 0.015 both agree. A second independent native speaker per language re-rated the 142 categorisable items blind; on the 121 determinate pairs the two humans agree with each other at κ = 0.659 (83.5% raw; bootstrap 95% CI [0.514, 0.785]) while each agrees with the inspector at κ ≤ 0.094. So the construct is not intrinsically unusable — what fails to reproduce is one automated instrument's application of it. Recoding the 21 "Unclear" responses does not change this (all-No → κ = 0.650; all-Yes → 0.467).
  3. The cause is the prompt, not the model (§IV-C). Re-grading the same 142 items with the identical model (Claude Sonnet 5) but the human instructions verbatim raises agreement with Human 2 from κ = 0.147 to κ = 0.636 on the 106 pairs it labels determinately — approaching the human–human reference of 0.659 — and its collapse rate matches the humans' (44.3% against 40.5–42.1%), with the directional over-attribution gone. Yet this re-prompted inspector agrees with the original inspector at only κ = 0.197 despite being the same model. The gap is attributable to four identifiable prompt asymmetries (§VI): the inspector's definition adds "or invented"; it is asked to search for evidence of every mode on every transcript while humans answered one yes/no question conditioned on a prior incorrectness judgement; humans got an illustrative example and the inspector did not; and the inspector was told to judge against the reference answer.

The paper's best single lesson is a worked case of a benchmark defect laundered into a failure-mode statistic (§IV-C, §IV-D, Table IV). Chasing the directional gap (inspector labels Collapse 1.81× as often as native speakers pooled) and stratifying it by round showed the factor is 1.17 outside one two-problem round and 4.18 inside it. Inspecting that round found that the English clause "13 puppies with spots" — the clause that places all 702 of Ibrahim's puppies in the numerator — is deleted from the Bengali and Chinese translations of both items, leaving a question and a reference answer that are mutually incompatible: the reference requires 801/900 = 89%, the corrupted text supports 99/900 = 11%, and both languages score 0.0% on that round across all twelve models. The second reviewer, unprompted, reconstructed the defect exactly in a free-text note. Across the 32 Bengali/Chinese instances the inspector assigns Collapse in 27; the two human rounds assign it in 4 and 8. Three further translation defects are documented (Chinese apartment-vacancy item changing the answer from 40 to 500; Thai dropping the "than the other N combined" comparison clause on both items; Chinese Round 1 duplicating a rate clause), plus the honest limit of their detector: numeric-token multiset comparison catches deleted and duplicated quantities but cannot catch a deleted attribute attached to a quantity, because the numbers survive — and two language-agnostic extensions they tried were unusable.

The four cheap checks the paper recommends (§V), in its own ordering, each with its stated cost:

  1. Permute the tie-break — if categories are assigned by ordered tests, re-run with the order reversed; if the plurality moves, report the ordering-invariant quantity instead. Cost: one pass over existing verdicts.
  2. Report the co-occurrence rate — the fraction of items carrying multiple labels bounds how much a single-label distribution can mean. Cost: none.
  3. Obtain a human sample, and cover the hard cases — coverage matters as much as size: their own six-language interim sample gave κ = 0.280 with nearly matching marginals (62.1% inspector vs 58.6% human) and no directional gap at all, purely because the languages that returned first were the ones models performed best on.
  4. Trace a directional gap to its items before reporting it as a rate — cost: one stratified split of an agreement table. Their words: "Had we reported the pooled figure alone we would have published a benchmark defect as a property of LLM judges."

Distinctness from the carded arxiv-2611-14581-evaluator-disagreement-commit-boundary (checked by grep before ranking; 2611.13140 appears nowhere in the live catalog). 2611.14581 asks which evaluator catches forbidden writes in agent episodes on tau2-bench and finds a minimal rule-based commit-boundary check beats LLM judges. 2611.13140 asks a prior, different question: whether a reported failure-mode share is measurable at all, and finds it is largely an artifact of classifier test order plus an unreproduced instrument. Different construct (aggregate taxonomy statistics vs per-episode outcome verification), different domain (multilingual grade-school math transcripts vs agent tool-write episodes), different failure (label assignment not reproducing across raters vs judges passing forbidden writes). I also checked the crowded adjacent space in the catalog — arxiv-2610-30491-trace-label-behavioral-detector-audit, arxiv-2610-13303-rubric-clarification-judge-consensus-not-human-agreement, arxiv-2609-22512-agreement-overstates-evidence-judge-consensus, arxiv-2611-04507-signals-agent-diagnostics, arxiv-2608-30391-autotracegt-grounded-theory-trajectory-codebooks — and none makes the tie-break-order point.

Limits I am flagging rather than smoothing over (§VI, Appendix B, Appendix C — all read). Single author, no venue, and code and data are not yet public: Appendix C says they "will be released publicly" and are "available from the author on request in the meantime" (the 179-item sample is SHA-256 content-hashed, digest prefix e75fd481). The panel is a self-described convenience sample and is confounded: API and local models are perfectly separated by accuracy (lowest API 67.18% > highest local 54.04%), parameter count does not predict accuracy (r = 0.025, p = 0.95), local quantisation was not recorded, and reasoning post-training tracks accuracy far better than scale (43.0% vs 10.1% mean). Self-inspection: the fixed inspector (Claude Sonnet 5) is itself a panel model — 195 of the graded records are its own generations — and it is the only model whose plurality survives the tie-break permutation, with a dual-evidence rate of 10.9% against 30.5–72.3% elsewhere; a second panel model shares its vendor family. Reviewer and language are perfectly confounded (one reviewer per language per round), Telugu was reviewed by the author, and the Round-2 blind-ID key was lost and reconstructed by content matching against transcripts (with a script that aborts unless the rebuild reproduces the original statistics exactly — it does). There is no monolingual English control: English was never run through the twelve models, so the paper cannot establish these failures are specific to cross-lingual transfer. Breadth is narrow — eight problem structures, all grade-school math word problems, one self-defined category, one inspector instrument. No seed was set, so traces are not bit-reproducible. And critically, the authors themselves scope the transfer: "The general lesson that reported failure distributions can be artifacts of measurement procedure is a hypothesis for other taxonomies, not a demonstrated fact." An AI Disclosure states the author used Anthropic's Claude and Moonshot AI's Kimi to help draft and edit post-submission revisions, write analysis scripts, format the LaTeX, and check reported numbers against analysis outputs.

Six-week EV

arXiv:2611.13140 — high EV for BQAA over six weeks, and the cheapest kind of high EV: it is a checklist, not a system.

If BQAA ever renders a failure-mode breakdown for agents — "X% tool-selection errors, Y% reasoning errors, Z% format errors" per model or per agent — this paper says that chart's shares are suspect by default, in two specific and separately fixable ways. First, if the categoriser tests categories in a fixed order and returns on the first match, and if categories can co-occur (they will — 50.1% here), then the reported plurality is close to a restatement of the test order, and the honest quantity is the ordering-invariant "carries evidence for this mode at all." Second, a low judge–human agreement coefficient on a category is ambiguous evidence: it can mean the category is unusable or that your instrument is an outlier, and it takes a second independent human rater to tell those apart. That second distinction is the one most teams will skip, because a single human round looks sufficient and here it would have produced exactly the wrong conclusion — the authors say so explicitly: "without it we would have concluded that the construct itself was the problem."

Two further items with durable value inside six weeks:

  • A directional judge–human gap is a lead, not a finding. Their 1.81× over-attribution became 1.17× once a single two-problem round was removed, and that round contained a deleted translation clause. Any BQAA eval that reports "the judge over-flags X by N×" should stratify before publishing the number, because the stratification cost here was one split of an agreement table and it converted a claimed judge bias into a data defect. This is the same shape of error the catalog already tracks in arxiv-2610-30491-trace-label-behavioral-detector-audit ("audit the detector behind the score") — the two reinforce each other.
  • Sample coverage beats sample size for the human validation round. Their six-language interim sample looked clean (κ = 0.280, marginals 62.1% vs 58.6%, no directional gap) purely because the languages that returned first were the ones models did best on. For BQAA that maps directly onto validating an LLM judge on whichever agent traces came back first — which will be the fast, successful ones.

Discount for the limits: single author, no venue, artifacts not yet released, narrow domain (grade-school math, one taxonomy, one instrument), confounded panel, self-inspection unresolved, no English control. The authors scope the generalisation as a hypothesis. So the EV is in the four checks as a process change — each cheap, none requiring you to accept the paper's domain claims — not in adopting its numbers.

TL;DRs for BQAA

  • arXiv:2611.13140 (Academy NEW, reserve promotion): a reported failure-mode share can be a classifier artifact — categories co-occurred on 50.1% of failures and reversing two tests moved the "dominant" mode in 11 of 12 models, swinging its share from 26.4% to 76.4% across the six orderings.
  • Same paper, reliability: raters agreed on answer correctness at κ = 0.932 and on the failure category at κ = 0.093 — but two independent humans agreed with each other at κ = 0.659, so the category is reproducible and the instrument was the outlier.
  • Same paper, diagnosis: re-prompting the same model with the human instructions verbatim moved κ against Human 2 from 0.147 to 0.636 — the gap was the prompt, not the model. Four prompt asymmetries are enumerated in §VI.
  • Same paper, data-vs-judge: a 1.81× judge over-attribution became 1.17× after one stratified split, and that split exposed a deleted clause in two translations that made the question and the reference answer mutually incompatible. Stratify before you publish a judge-bias number.
  • Same paper, action for BQAA: adopt the four cheap checks (permute the tie-break; report co-occurrence; get a human sample that covers the hard cases; trace any directional gap to its items) before any failure-mode rate is compared across models or agents.
