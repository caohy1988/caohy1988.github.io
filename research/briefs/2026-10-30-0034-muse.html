#1 — Prefix Executability: Evaluating Tool-Using Agents Beyond Final Success — https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.40444

Rezaeian, Sun, Benajiba & Roth (Oracle AI). Full PDF read (all 21 pp. incl. Appendices A–G and Limitations).

The paper defines a trajectory-level evaluation protocol: replay an emitted tool-call sequence and find the first prefix that is not executable under tool schemas, state preconditions, and data dependencies. Its metrics are First-Error Depth (FED, Eq. 1), the fixed-cohort Prefix Executability Curve (PEC, Eq. 3–4), Trajectory Executability Rate (TER, Eq. 8), horizon-normalized AUC-PEC (Eq. 10–11), and Recovery Rate (RR, Eq. 9: final success conditional on having had an invalid prefix). The benchmark scorer is deterministic and uses no LLM judge (§2.1, Appendix C.1), credits any executable path rather than one reference trajectory, and labels each first failure with one of five causes — schema, dependency, precondition, state-consistency, information (§2.4). Exact identity Eq. 12 decomposes final success into uninterrupted execution plus recovery: Pr(success) = q·s + (1−q)·r with q = TER.

Evidence: 6,768 rollouts over 564 tasks per model–method condition (§2.6) — stratified 200-task samples of BFCL-v3 multi-turn and ToolSandbox, plus all 164 Airline/Retail base-split tasks of τ³-bench — across three model conditions and four agent variants (ReAct, Prompt Planning, Plan-Conditioned ReAct, and Dual-Loop, a planner-verifier validation probe). Headline divergence (Table 2): under the strongest model condition on ToolSandbox, Plan-Conditioned ReAct has the highest final success (0.69) but TER 0.48 and AUC-PEC 0.52, versus Dual-Loop at 0.94 and 0.95 with final success 0.63. The extreme case (§3.1): on BFCL-v3 with the weakest model, Plan-Conditioned ReAct is fully executable in only 10 of 200 episodes (TER 0.05) yet succeeds in 148 — 138 of those 148 successes (93.2%) passed through an earlier invalid prefix. Pre-execution plan validation (Dual-Loop) consistently restores prefix reliability on BFCL-v3 and ToolSandbox at competitive terminal success.

The boundary is stated honestly and matters for adoption: the validation advantage does not transfer to the interactive τ³-bench audit (Table F.1; with the strongest model, plain ReAct leads TER/AUC-PEC at 0.95/0.95 while Plan-Conditioned leads final success at 0.51), and the paired verifier-sensitivity study finds all TER/AUC-PEC deltas' 95% intervals include zero — e.g. the strongest verifier at budget 5 changes TER by +0.006 [−0.018, +0.030] while adding 40.7k tokens and 176.9s per task (Table F.3). Limitations (§Limitations, p. 9): audit-scale samples, no within-task repeated-seed variation, executability assumes explicit schemas/preconditions and benchmark-accessible state, no matched schema-constrained-decoding baseline, and executability does not certify semantic correctness.

Why it ranks for BQAA: FED/TER/PEC/RR are exactly the kind of per-trajectory columns a warehouse of tool-call rows can carry — first-invalid-step depth, first-failure cause, recovered-or-not — computed by deterministic replay against registered schemas and state, with no judge in the loop. "Success rate" dashboards that do not split uninterrupted success from recovered success will systematically over-credit plan-conditioned agents that emit invalid intermediate structure; Eq. 12 is the corrective decomposition, and Table 2 shows the ranking flips are large, not marginal.

#2 — Judged in Company: In-Context Batching Makes LLM Verdicts Depend on Other Items — https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.40241

Xie & Alanwar (Technical University of Munich). Full PDF read (all 33 pp. incl. Appendices A–J, Limitations, and proofs).

Batching many items into one judge prompt assumes separability: an item's verdict does not depend on its neighbours or its slot. Grading the same 2,000 items per task alone and in three independent random partitions of 25, across three tasks (harm in social-media comments, short-answer grading, review star ratings) and five open-weight 4–9B models, a new random partition changes 11–22% of decisions on the two ambiguous tasks — 6.5–13.3 points above the largest control (renamed keys, reworded instruction, or one-at-a-time generation noise at 0.5–2.1%; Table 2) — while AUROC against human labels moves only −0.033 to +0.010 (Table 3). Four 14–32B models repeat the core design: 5.9–14.6% of decisions change, 4.2–9.1 points above baseline (Table 16). On the easy star-rating task (AUROC 0.96) only 2.9–3.9% of decisions change, which the authors treat as the boundary condition: the effect is large where the judgement is ambiguous (§5.6).

The formal core is what makes this more than a bias demo. Flip rates decompose exactly into neighbour-order, slot, membership, and slot×membership components (Proposition 2, Fig. 2, Table 10): order carries 0.42–0.69 of the latent dissimilarity, slot 0.09–0.32, membership 0.01–0.25. Under a threshold model, flips grow with the square root of context-contributed variance (Proposition 3) — at the median, 1% of latent variance flips 4.5% of decisions — which is why accuracy checks approve batching while one decision in seven changes (§5.2). Mitigations mostly fail as measured: majority votes over nine permutations of a batch still leave 52–60% of the flips (Table 17); the majority of fifteen independent batches still disagrees with single-item grading on 8.4–17.8% of items (Table 18); threshold recalibration removes only the operating-point part. Within-generation edits show both channels act — swapping in another batch's earlier verdicts changes 7–23% of target decisions, swapping neighbour texts 9–26% (Fig. 3, Table 7). Appendix G reports where the effect was first seen: a production Chinese-moderation system using a closed, probability-returning model the paper identifies as Jev 1.13 at 16 comments per request, where batching changed 9.2% (COLD) and 10.7% (ToxiCN) of decisions versus per-item requests, and averaging six batches still left 10–24% of borderline decisions changed for that model and three open models. Limitations: main models are small open-weight models (proprietary models may differ), three English tasks, one prompt template, greedy decoding; the Appendix-G closed model answers per item against a shared context, so it does not test the verdict channel; several latent-scale quantities rest on the Gaussian threshold model, whose misfits the paper reports.

Why it ranks for BQAA: any evaluation or trace-scoring pipeline that batches judge calls to cut cost — including decision-model judges — is measuring with an instrument whose per-item verdicts depend on batch composition, and aggregate accuracy validation will not catch it. The operational takeaways are concrete: grade ambiguous items one per prompt (cached-prefix economics in §6 make this cheaper than it looks), and where batching stays, log batch id, slot, and composition with every verdict and report flip-vs-single-item rates alongside accuracy. For an analytics store, batch composition is provenance, not plumbing.

#3 — Soei: Structured Observability of Evidence and Intent for Multi-Agent AI Software Engineering — https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.39468

Khan & Kumar (Intel). Full PDF read (all 25 pp. incl. Appendices A–E and threats-to-validity discussion).

Soei proposes an evidence substrate for coding-agent observability — acknowledgement-aware event schema (a tool call is "proposed" at start and only rendered once its completion acknowledges it, §5.2), an ingest-time privacy gate before persistence, a hash-chained local evidence ledger, a W3C-PROV-aligned evidence graph, and a verification layer that separates an agent's completion claim from independent proof (§5.6, Algorithm 2). The empirical pilot runs a stdlib-only prototype over 45 real VS Code Copilot agent sessions (102,233 records; 20,577 tool executions; 893 code-editing episodes; §9.2). With a rule-based proof classifier whose labels were blind-audited across four versions (final audit precision 83.3% for "unverified", 96.7% for "verified", n=30 each; Appendix D), the bias-corrected estimate is that 23.4% [95% CI 17.1–35.7] of code-editing episodes end with no independent check after the last edit (Table 7, Eq. 4), and 186 of 322 to-dos the agent marked complete after editing (57.8%) had no proof between edit and completion. When agents do verify, formal tests are only 4.5% of verification actions; running the changed artifact and UI checks dominate (§10.1, Finding 3). Two further measurements generalize beyond coding: a 10-minute timing heuristic recovers only 6 of 24 session pairs that content lineage links, because median real cross-session reuse is 8.8 days later (Table 8); and 1,326 raw records across 16 of 45 sessions contain credential-like strings, versus zero in the gated ledger (§10.6). The ledger itself is 20% of raw transcript size and verifies at ~126k events/s with deterministic replay and tamper detection (Table 9).

Threats, stated by the authors (§11): one developer, one agent product, post-silicon-validation context — an existence-and-magnitude pilot, not population rates; the proof classifier detects that a check ran, not whether it passed (Table 14, item 1: failed checks currently count as proof); lineage is path-level, not content-digest-level; "verified" means a check ran after the last edit, not that the code is correct. The architecture and governance framing (§13–14: autonomy levels gated on harness metrics such as unverified hand-back rate and claim precision) is proposal, not evidence. Note the arXiv listing abstract additionally describes a source-level verification of a third-party observability tool; the PDF read for this card (preprint v1.2, 29 Oct 2026) does not contain that study, and no claim from it is used here.

Why it ranks for BQAA: claim-vs-proof is a computable separation over session rows — completion claims (agent-reported) joined against later proof events (test/build/exec/probe) with acknowledgement pairing so failed or never-completed tool calls are not shown as applied work (1.1% of mutations here were requested but never succeeded, §10.5). BQAA already stores the raw material; this paper supplies the metric definitions (unverified hand-back rate, claim precision, phantom rate, lineage coverage; Table 11), realistic baselines from real sessions, and the warning that session-linking by timing proximity will miss three quarters of real hand-offs — lineage has to key on artifact identity.

Six-week EV

  • Add first-invalid-step columns to trajectory views (from #1). Cheapest high-value change: for each stored tool-call trajectory, record whether every prefix was executable against the registered tool schemas, the depth and cause (schema / dependency / precondition / state / information) of the first invalid call, and whether final success was uninterrupted or recovered.   • Audit every batched judge call in our own pipelines (from #2). Inventory where evaluation, trace scoring, or moderation-style classification sends multiple items per prompt — decision-model judges included, per Appendix G's production observation.   • Split "claimed done" from "proved done" in session analytics (from #3).   • Watch the ScopeDB pattern, not the post.
